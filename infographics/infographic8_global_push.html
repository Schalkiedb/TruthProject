🇺🇸 United States — Political Push

Washington · 2023–2026

On January 8, 2026, the Heritage Foundation — the organisation that authored Project 2025, over 50% of which has already been implemented by the Trump administration — released a companion policy document titled “Saving America by Saving the Family.” Framed as a “culture-wide Manhattan Project” to avert demographic collapse before America’s 250th anniversary, the report’s most controversial proposal calls for the restoration of Blue Laws to establish a “uniform day of rest” on Sundays.

The legal strategy cites the 1961 Supreme Court case McGowan v. Maryland, which upheld Sunday closing laws as constitutional labour regulations. However, the report’s own language links Sunday rest to combating “spiritual homelessness” and encouraging “religious observance” — potentially undermining the secular framing. The mechanism: amending the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) to require time-and-a-half overtime pay for Sunday workers, creating economic pressure to close without a direct mandate.

“A uniform day of rest… can provide temporal boundaries that help communities set aside time for religious observance, family gatherings, outdoor activities, and rest.”

— Heritage Foundation SR323, Page 38, January 8, 2026

Heritage Foundation · Mandate for Leadership · 2023

The Sunday rest proposal appeared on page 589 of Project 2025 using explicitly theological language: “God ordained the Sabbath as a day of rest, and until recent years, the Judeo-Christian tradition sought to honor that mandate by moral and legal regulation.” The January 2026 special report elevated this from an obscure appendix item to a front-line policy priority.

Status: In active advocacy — no legislation enacted as of March 2026

Status: Active opposition — First Amendment attorneys on standby

🇪🇺 European Union — Sunday Alliance & Vatican

Brussels · 2011–2026

Founded in 2011 at the European Parliament in Brussels, the European Sunday Alliance (ESA) is a network of over 100 organisations — trade unions, employers’ groups, Catholic family associations, environmental groups, and churches — lobbying for Sunday to become a legally protected, work-free day across all 27 EU member states.

On March 1, 2024, ahead of the June 2024 EU Elections, the ESA issued a new manifesto calling on all MEP candidates to commit to Sunday protection. Within days, MEPs from four political groups endorsed it: S&D, EPP, Greens/EFA, and Renew Europe.

Religious Lobby

COMECE — Catholic Bishops’ EU Commission

Founding ESA Member · Vatican’s EU Political Arm

COMECE (Commission of the Bishops’ Conferences of the European Union) is the institutional Catholic Church’s lobbying body at the EU. As a founding member and key infrastructure provider for the ESA, it connects the EU Sunday push directly to Vatican policy. The institutional line runs: Constantine’s Sunday law (321 AD) → Council of Laodicea (364 AD) → COMECE’s 2024 EU Parliament lobbying.

Status: Active, well-funded, cross-party MEP endorsements secured June 2024

Labour Law

EU Right to Disconnect Directive

France (2016) · Belgium (2023) · EU Commission Pending

Running parallel to the religious Sunday push is the secular “Right to Disconnect” movement — workers’ legal right to ignore work communications outside official hours. France passed it in 2016, Belgium in 2023, Italy proposed it, and the EU Commission faces growing pressure to issue a binding EU-wide directive. An EU survey shows workers at home are 6× more likely to work in free time.

Status: France & Belgium have law; EU-wide directive under active pressure from trade unions

Sunday Trading Restrictions — EU Existing Law

Germany · Austria · Poland · Expanding 2024–25

Most of Western Europe already has significant Sunday trading restrictions. Germany’s Ladenschlussgesetz closes most shops on Sundays. Poland extended Sunday restrictions in November 2024 legislation. The ESA’s strategy: use existing national Sunday laws as the foundation for EU-wide harmonisation — making the exception the rule across all 27 member states.

What Critics Are Warning
⚠ The Three Key Objections
The Saturday-Keeper Problem

Any law mandating Sunday as a “uniform rest day” places an extra economic burden on the 70M+ Christians who observe Saturday, plus all Jewish (Friday sunset–Saturday sunset), Muslim (Friday), and other faiths. Businesses observing their own Sabbath must close twice — losing two days of revenue against Sunday-observing competitors who close only once. Heritage’s document acknowledges this but dismisses it, stating Sunday is the only “realistic” choice for the majority.

The First Amendment Question

The Heritage document’s own language may be its legal Achilles heel. By explicitly linking Sunday rest to combating “spiritual homelessness” and encouraging “religious observance,” the authors potentially violate the Establishment Clause — the very standard used in McGowan v. Maryland (1961) to uphold Sunday laws only because they were framed as secular labour regulations. A 2026 Sunday law citing religious motivation faces an entirely different constitutional challenge.

160 Years of Institutional Memory

The Seventh-day Adventist Church’s memory runs back to the 1880s–1890s, when American Adventists were imprisoned and sentenced to chain-gang labour under state Blue Laws. They have maintained First Amendment lawyers, published Liberty magazine, and opposed Sunday legislation continuously for 160 years. Their formal statement calls SR323 “a foot-in-the-door toward influencing states to legislatively enact restrictive worship laws.”
