Era I — Pagan Rome's War on the Sabbath
135 AD – 313 AD
Emperor Hadrian's 135 AD edict banning Sabbath observance across the Roman Empire was the first state law in history to target Saturday worship. It was driven by anti-Jewish politics following the Bar Kokhba revolt — not theology. Jewish and Sabbath-keeping Gentile Christians alike were targeted. The penalty: death. The communities: scattered but not destroyed.

Fate: Mass execution, enslavement, exile from Jerusalem. Sabbath banned by imperial decree.

Nazarene Communities of Asia Minor
2nd–4th century
Jewish Christians who kept both Torah and faith in Jesus — including the Sabbath. Documented by Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375 AD) as still keeping Saturday in Asia Minor. Persecuted by Roman authorities and increasingly marginalised by Sunday-keeping Gentile churches.
Fate: Systematic marginalisation; communities eventually dispersed. Epiphanius calls them neither fully Jewish nor fully Christian — but acknowledges their Saturday observance continued into the 4th century.

Christians of Gaul — Letter from the Churches
c. 177 AD
Persecuted Christians in Gaul (modern France) under Marcus Aurelius wrote: "None can fully set forth the weight of our misery, the madness and rage of the Gentiles against the saints… their feet were dragged out and compressed to the utmost tension… several suffocated in prison." Sabbath was described as "the only comfort in their hard lives and the steps towards the kingdom of heaven."
Fate: Massacred in Lyon 177 AD. Blandina and companions tortured to death. Their letter preserved in Eusebius. The Sabbath comfort testimony survived.

Churches of Asia Minor (Polycarp Tradition)
2nd–3rd century
Eastern churches — Smyrna, Ephesus, Sardis — observed Saturday as primary worship day alongside Sunday. Rejected Roman Easter dating. Victor of Rome (c. 195 AD) attempted to excommunicate all Asian churches for their different calendar. They refused to comply.
Fate: Victor's excommunication attempt failed — Irenaeus of Lyon publicly rebuked him. The East maintained independence. The refusal to submit to Rome on calendar matters preserved the Saturday tradition for two more centuries.

Era II — Constantine, the Councils & the Forced Transition
313 AD – 600 AD
The Council of Laodicea (364 AD) Canon 29 was the institutional death warrant for Saturday worship: "Christians shall not Judaize and be idle on Saturday… but shall work on that day; but the Lord's day they shall especially honour." Yet Socrates Scholasticus wrote in 440 AD that "almost all churches throughout the world celebrate the sacred mysteries on the Sabbath of every week." The law and the reality diverged completely.
Condemned
Saturday-Keepers in Rome (c. 600 AD)
c. 590–604 AD
A class of Sabbath-keeping Christians existed in the city of Rome itself — strict enough in their Saturday observance to anger Pope Gregory I. He wrote against "Roman citizens who forbid doing any work on the Sabbath day" and called teachers of seventh-day rest "prophets of the Antichrist."
Fate: Pope Gregory's decree. The community scattered. Their existence in Rome itself, 250 years after Constantine, proves the suppression was never total.
Rome, Italy

Hypsistari / Sabbath-Keepers of Cappadocia
4th century
A sect in Cappadocia (modern Turkey) who combined Jewish and Christian practices including Saturday Sabbath, rejecting idols and sacrifices. Documented by Gregory of Nazianzus, whose own father had been a member before converting. Gregory describes them as those who "honour the Sabbath."
Fate: Condemned by local councils. Gregory's description is one of the few sympathetic accounts of a Sabbath-keeping community in 4th-century Christian literature.
Cappadocia (modern Turkey)

Ewostatewos of Ethiopia (1273–1352)
1273–1352 AD
Ethiopian monk who championed the Saturday Sabbath within the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Defended it to the Patriarch of Alexandria in person. When Abuna Yaqob opposed his views, he left Ethiopia — visiting Sudan, Cairo, Jerusalem, Cyprus, and finally settling in Armenia where he died in exile.
Fate: Voluntary exile to Armenia where he died September 23, 1352. His followers in Ethiopia were persecuted and confined to border regions. VINDICATED 98 years later: Emperor Zara Yaqob forced the Coptic Church to concede Saturday observance at the Council of Dabra Metmaq (1450). Canonised as a saint. His cause is still embodied in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church's dual-Sabbath practice today.
Ethiopia / Armenia

Arian Christians
4th–5th century
A major 4th-century Christian movement. Socrates Scholasticus documents (Book 6, ch. 8) that Arians in Constantinople held Saturday assemblies alongside Sunday assemblies — maintaining both days. Their Saturday assemblies were "performed with great solemnity." When the Emperor expelled them from the city, they worshipped outside the walls — still keeping Saturday.
Fate: Condemned as heretical at Nicaea for theological reasons unrelated to the Sabbath. Their documented Saturday practice survived in Socrates' historical record — proving the dual-day tradition was mainstream, not fringe, in the 4th-5th century Eastern church.
Constantinople

Era III — The Celtic Church & Dark Ages Survivors
400 AD – 1100 AD
The Celtic church — Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Iona, Iceland, the Faroes — kept Saturday for five centuries outside Rome's direct reach. Prof. James Moffat (Princeton): "It seems to have been customary in the Celtic churches to keep Saturday as a day of rest from labour." Prof. Andrew Lang: "They worked on Sunday, but kept Saturday in a Sabbatical manner." When Rome finally extended control, the installation of a Roman bishop was the mechanism of suppression — not military force.
Preserved — 500 Years
St. Patrick (c. 387–461 AD)
c. 387–461 AD
Ireland's patron saint, trained in the Celtic tradition of Saturday Sabbath. His own Confession contains no reference to Rome's authority over the calendar. Prof. Andrew Lang: Celtic Christians "worked on Sunday, but kept Saturday in a Sabbatical manner." Stories attributing Sunday-keeping to Patrick were written by Probus and Joscelyn c. 1130 AD — 500 years after his death — as retrospective claim-staking by Rome.
Fate: Died peacefully. His Saturday-keeping tradition survived in Ireland for 600+ more years. Rome's counter-narrative written 5 centuries later is the admission that the original needed covering.

St. Columba of Iona (c. 521–597 AD)
c. 521–597 AD
Founded the monastery of Iona (563 AD) — the spiritual centre of Celtic Christianity. Deathbed words, June 9, 597 AD: "This day is called the Sabbath, that is, the rest day, and it is truly the Sabbath to me, for it will put an end to my labours." Butler's Lives of the Saints records this directly. From Iona, Celtic missionaries carried Saturday observance to Iceland and the Faroe Islands.
Fate: Died peacefully at Iona on a Saturday. His tradition survived for 500+ more years in the British Isles, Iceland, and the Faroe Islands before Rome's bishops arrived to suppress it.

Era IV — The Medieval Inquisition: Fire and Sword
1100 AD – 1500 AD
Pope Lucius III's 1184 bull Ad Abolendam and Pope Innocent III's launch of the Albigensian Crusade (1209) institutionalised the suppression of Sabbath-adjacent movements. The Third Lateran Council (1179) condemned the Passagini by name. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) mandated civic cooperation in hunting heretics. For 300 years, holding a different view of the Sabbath carried the death penalty across most of Europe.

The Passagini (Passagii / Insabbatati) — Northern Italy
c. 1150–1260 AD
A distinct Sabbatarian movement in Lombardy — among the earliest groups to articulate sola scriptura. Kept the seventh-day Sabbath, refused circumcision of theology with Catholic practice. Named by Pope Lucius III's 1184 bull. Inquisitor Bonacursus (c. 1190) documented their Sabbath theology in detail: "Not a few, but many know what the errors of those called Passagini are." Condemned by the Third Lateran Council (1179) by name. Targeted in the Albigensian Crusade alongside Cathari and Waldenses.
Fate: Eradicated through the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the medieval Inquisition. No surviving institutional trace. Their theology was preserved only in the writings of their enemies. The Inquisitor's documentation of their Sabbath belief is the clearest proof that Saturday observance was their defining practice.
Lombardy, Northern Italy

The Cathari / Albigenses — Southern France
1209–1350 AD
A major Christian reform movement in southern France and northern Italy. Catholic inquisitors documented their Sabbath-adjacent practices including rejection of Sunday feast days as papal inventions. Pope Innocent III launched the Albigensian Crusade in 1209. At the sack of Béziers (July 22, 1209), the papal legate was asked how to distinguish Cathars from Catholics. Reported response: "Kill them all — God will know his own." Estimated 20,000+ killed in one day. Raphael Lemkin (who coined "genocide") cited this as "one of the most conclusive cases of genocide in religious history."
Fate: The Albigensian Crusade and subsequent Inquisition killed tens of thousands and eradicated all discernible traces of Catharism by 1350. The last known Cathar perfectus, Guilhem Bélibaste, was burned at Villerouge-Termenès in 1321.
Languedoc, Southern France

The Waldensians (Valdesi / Insabbatati) — Alps
c. 1173 – Ongoing
Named "Insabbatati" by Catholic inquisitors — Sabbath-keepers. Jesuit Inquisitor Francis Pegne cited their Sabbath observance "according to the custom of Jews." 12th-century Inquisitor Moneta of Cremona "railed against the Waldenses for seventh day sabbath keeping." The Piedmontese Easter massacre (April 24, 1655): 1,712 killed in one day in the Waldensian valleys. Oliver Cromwell raised funds across Protestant Europe for survivors. Six centuries of continuous persecution — crusades 1209, 1487; massacres 1488, 1545, 1655.
Fate: 600 years of attempted extermination — crusades, massacres, Inquisition — failed to destroy them. The Waldensian Church (Chiesa Valdese) exists to this day, merged with the Italian Methodist Church in 1975, as the living institutional descendant of medieval Sabbatarians.

c. 1520s–1599
Hungarian nobleman who became the leader of the Sabbatarian movement in Transylvania. Era VI — England: Pilloried, Imprisoned, Hanged, and Drawn
1617 AD – 1700 AD
England produced the most documented individual case histories of Sabbath persecution in the 17th century. The Star Chamber, the Court of High Commission, and the laws against Dissenters combined to make Saturday observance a prosecutable offence. Eleven Sabbatarian churches flourished in England by 1700 — despite continuous persecution.

John Trask — London
c. 1585–1636
Began teaching seventh-day Sabbath in London c. 1617. Brought before the infamous Star Chamber — Archbishop Laud presiding. Bishop Andrews introduced a new argument there: that early martyrs were tested by the question "Hast thou kept the Lord's Day?" — the first use of this argument in history.
Fate: "Sentenced, on account of his being a Sabbatarian, to be set upon the pillory at Westminster, and from thence to be whipped to the Fleet prison" — about two miles of public whipping. Imprisoned for three years. Eventually recanted under duress. His wife Dorothy refused to recant.
London, England

Wife of John Trask. When her husband recanted, she refused. Her "crime": continuing to observe the seventh-day Sabbath and observe Old Testament dietary laws. Kept in Fleet prison for fifteen years for refusing to abandon Saturday observance. Described by contemporaries as a woman of exceptional courage and conviction.
Fate: Fifteen years in Fleet prison. Died in prison — still refusing to recant. One of the most sustained individual imprisonments for Sabbath observance in English history. Her husband's recantation is remembered; her death in prison is not.

Pastor of London's seventh-day Mill Yard congregation. A poor silk weaver. Era VII — The New World: Suppression in the Land of Liberty
1664 AD – 1888 AD
Stephen Mumford arrived in Newport, Rhode Island in 1664 — three years after John James's execution — carrying Sabbatarian belief to the New World. By 1671 the first Seventh Day Baptist church in America was founded at Newport. By 1863 the Seventh-day Adventist Church was organised with 3,500 members. By 1888, a national Sunday Rest Bill came within one senator's vote of making Saturday-keeping economically suicidal across the entire United States.
Founded New World Tradition
Stephen Mumford — Newport, RI
c. 1639–1707
First Sabbath-keeper in America. Arrived from London 1664, three years after witnessing or being shaped by John James's martyrdom. Brought the conviction that "the whole of the ten commandments as delivered from Sinai are moral and immutable; and that it was the Antichristian power which thought to change times and laws that changed the Sabbath from the seventh to the first day."
Fate: Lived and died peacefully in Newport. Founded a community that grew into the Seventh Day Baptist General Conference (1802) and, through Rachel Oakes Preston (1801–1868), seeded the Saturday conviction that became the Seventh-day Adventist Church.
Newport, Rhode Island

Fate: Thirteen men and women documented on chain gangs in the nation founded on religious liberty. The cases generated the founding of what became Liberty magazine (1886) — still published today. Precedent — 46 Years of Harm
TWA v. Hardison (US Supreme Court, 1977)
1977–2023
The US Supreme Court ruled 7–2 that employers need only make a "de minimis" (trivially minimal) effort to accommodate religious Sabbath observance. For 46 years this made it legally easy for any employer to refuse Saturday-keeping employees any accommodation. Hundreds of thousands of Sabbath-keepers in the US workforce faced the choice: abandon Saturday or lose employment.
Fate: 46 years of legal suppression by a Supreme Court ruling that made Sabbath accommodation economically optional. OVERTURNED: Groff v. DeJoy (June 29, 2023) — unanimous ruling by the US Supreme Court. New standard: "substantial increased costs." The SDA General Conference and NAD filed amicus briefs. 46 years reversed in one decision.
