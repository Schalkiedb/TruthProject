Athinganoi / "Athenians" (Touch-Not) Verified
8th–12th Century AD · Phrygia · Byzantine Empire
Eastern Sabbath-keeping sect — confirmed by Cardinal Hergenrother
An Eastern body of Sabbath-keeping Christians documented from the 8th century to the 12th, known as the Athinganoi (Greek: "those who do not touch") or "touch-not" — named for their strict purity practices. Neander documented them: "This sect, which had its principal seat in the city of Armorion, in upper Phrygia, where many Jews resided, sprung out of a mixture of Judaism and Christianity. They united baptism with the observance of all the rites of Judaism, circumcision excepted." Crucially, Cardinal Hergenrother — a senior Catholic Church authority — confirmed that they "stood in intimate relation with Emperor Michael II (AD 821–829) and observed the Sabbath." The 10th and 11th centuries saw great extension of these Eastern sects from Asia Minor to Western Europe — making them the probable bridge community between the Paulicians and the medieval Waldensian-era Sabbatarians. Their documented relationship with a Byzantine emperor is particularly significant.

St. Patrick & the Celtic Church of Ireland Verified
c. 387–461 AD · Ireland · Founded the Celtic Church of Ireland
Founding missionary of Irish Christianity — his church kept Saturday
What is well-documented: Four independent scholarly sources confirm the Celtic Church Patrick founded kept Saturday as the Sabbath. (1) Prof. James C. Moffatt, D.D. (Princeton), The Church in Scotland, 1882, p. 140: "It seems to have been customary in the Celtic churches of early times, in Ireland as well as Scotland, to keep Saturday, the Jewish Sabbath, as a day of rest from labor." (2) W.T. Skene, Adamnan Life of St. Columba, p. 96: "We find traces in the early monastic church of Ireland that they held Saturday to be the Sabbath on which they rested from all their labours." (3) A.C. Flick, The Rise of the Medieval Church, p. 237: "The Celts kept Saturday as a day of rest." (4) Catholic historian T. Ratcliffe Barnett, Margaret of Scotland: Queen and Saint, p. 97: "The ancient Irish Church observed Saturday instead of Sunday as the day of rest." Even a Catholic historian confirms it.

What Patrick's own writings show: Patrick's two authenticated writings — Confession and Epistle to Coroticus — contain no reference to Sunday observance, no mention of Rome's authority, no appeal to any papal commission. He never mentions a pope. He never cites a church council. He grounds everything solely in Scripture and a direct call from God. He was born into a family where his father was a deacon and grandfather a presbyter — a Celtic (not Roman Catholic) family structure. McClintock and Strong, Cyclopedia of Biblical, Theological, and Ecclesiastical Literature, Vol. VII, p. 776: "He was no Romanist. His life and evangelical Church of the 5th century ought to be better known."

The Rome fabrication — 700 years later: The Catholic priest Jocelyn of Furness (writing c. 1130 AD — 669 years after Patrick's death) was the first to insert a Catholic background into Patrick's story. No writer before Jocelyn connected Patrick to papal authority. "Two centuries elapsed after Patrick's death before any writer attempted to connect Patrick's work with a papal commission." (ibid.) The entire "Patrick was Catholic and kept Sunday" narrative was constructed by a single medieval writer more than six centuries after the events. By contrast, the Sabbath-keeping of the church he founded is attested by four independent scholarly sources including a Catholic historian.

Evidentiary caution: No surviving document in Patrick's own hand specifically names Saturday as his day of rest. The evidence is strong institutional inference: he founded and led the Celtic Church of Ireland for decades, and that church is independently confirmed to have kept Saturday. The silence of his own writings on Sunday is notable given that defending Sunday would have been a natural response to Celtic-Roman disputes.
