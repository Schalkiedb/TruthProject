Why This Matters Theologically

If the Sabbath had been changed to Sunday by divine authority, we would expect the world's languages — many of which predate the Catholic Church — to reflect that change. Instead, the opposite is true. In over 105 languages across every inhabited continent, Saturday retains the name "Sabbath." Not a single language in the world calls Sunday "the Sabbath." The linguistic record is unambiguous: the peoples of the world understood Saturday to be the rest day. The only languages that use a pagan name for Saturday ("Saturn's Day") are those directly shaped by Roman planetary naming — the same Rome that issued the edicts to suppress it. The language is the evidence. The evidence is in the name.

The Implications — What the Languages Prove
I
The Name Predates the Change
Languages that developed independently of Rome — Arabic, Persian, Ethiopian, Georgian, Armenian — all call Saturday the Sabbath. These naming conventions were established long before any church council claimed the authority to change the day. The Sabbath identity of Saturday is older than Christianity itself.
II
Rome Could Change the Practice but Not the Name
Even in countries where the Catholic Church held total religious authority for centuries — Spain, Portugal, Italy, France, Poland, Romania — the name "Saturday" retained its Sabbath root. The Church could enforce Sunday worship by law, but it could not rename Saturday. The linguistic evidence survived where the communities did not.
III
Only Pagan Rome Erased the Name
The only language family where Saturday lost its Sabbath name is Germanic — where Roman planetary naming imposed "Saturn's Day." Yet even there, German "Samstag" preserves the Sabbath root, and Scandinavian "Lördag" preserves the Sabbath preparation custom. English is the most thoroughly paganized — and it is the language of the world's largest Sunday-keeping Protestant tradition. The correlation is not coincidental.
