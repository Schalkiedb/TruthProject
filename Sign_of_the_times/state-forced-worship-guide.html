Framework

The Two Tablets of the Law

Across Jewish and Christian tradition, the Ten Commandments are commonly read as falling into two groups: duties toward God (the "first tablet") and duties toward one's neighbor (the "second tablet"). Where exactly the line falls differs slightly by tradition.

2. No idols
3. No taking God's name in vain
4. Remember the Sabbath		1. No other gods / no idols (combined)

(The final commandment against coveting is split in two to reach ten total)

What follows traces four world empires across Scripture and history, each of which — on this reading — used state power to intervene in that first-tablet territory: matters of worship, loyalty, and allegiance to God, rather than civil crimes against a neighbor. Rome does this twice, in two very different costumes, which is why it gets a section of its own.

A Necessary Qualification

Why Only the First Tablet Counts Here

It's worth pausing on why this document tracks first-tablet violations specifically, rather than persecution or state violence in general. Most historical atrocity — conquest, ethnic conflict, seizure of land or property, political purges — falls under the second tablet: theft, murder, false witness, coveting. That kind of state violence is real, common, and thoroughly documented across every empire in history, including all four discussed here. It simply isn't what this document is about.

What distinguishes the episodes collected below is narrower and more specific: a government compelling or forbidding a particular act of worship, under civil or capital penalty, rather than regulating conduct between people. Nebuchadnezzar's furnace, Darius's lions, Antiochus's Sabbath ban, Decius's libelli, and Charlemagne's Sunday courts are not being cited as examples of "persecution" in a generic sense — plenty of that has nothing to do with the first tablet — but as a specific, recurring device: the state as guarantor of a particular answer to the question of who, or what, is to be worshiped.

Scripture's own distinction

This isn't a distinction imposed on the text from outside. Jesus himself separates the two categories when summarizing the law as love of God and love of neighbor (Matthew 22:37–40) — two commandments, not one — and Paul does the same in Romans 13:9, listing only second-tablet commands ("you shall not murder... steal... covet") as being "summed up" in love of neighbor, leaving the first tablet as a distinct category of its own. The two-tablet division isn't a later theological gloss; it runs through the text these episodes are drawn from.

Pattern Recognition

The Pattern: Four Kingdoms, One Tablet

In the historicist prophetic tradition — the interpretive lineage running through the Reformers and into Millerite/Adventist reading of Daniel and Revelation — the four world empires of Daniel's visions (Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome) are read as a sequence, each one at some point turning the machinery of the state against the first tablet of the law. The commandment broken differs by empire, but the shape of the act — a decree, backed by the threat of death, demanding or forbidding an act of worship — repeats each time.

A note before the details

This is a real and old line of biblical interpretation, not something invented for this document. But it's worth being honest about its shape: it is a thematic pattern — state coercion of worship — not a claim that these four episodes are one single continuous conspiracy. Sincere, informed Christians disagree on how tightly the "four kingdoms" scheme points to any single future fulfillment versus describing something that has simply recurred, in different forms, many times across history.

Nebuchadnezzar erects a golden image, ninety feet tall, on the plain of Dura and issues a decree: when the royal orchestra sounds, every nation, tribe, and language is to fall down and worship the image, on pain of being cast into a furnace of blazing fire (Daniel 3:4–6). This is state coercion in its most direct form — no ambiguity, no gradual drift, just a decree and an execution method attached to it.

Which commandments it breaks: The decree violates the first commandment by demanding worship of something other than God, and the second by requiring homage to a manufactured image — the very two things Exodus 20:3–5 forbids in successive verses. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego's refusal (Daniel 3:16–18) is framed in the text as precisely a first-tablet stand: not treason against the king in any ordinary sense, but a refusal to transfer worship due to God alone.

A secondary episode reinforces the same empire's disregard for the sacred: Belshazzar's feast (Daniel 5), where the king drinks wine from vessels looted from the Jerusalem temple while praising "gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone" (Daniel 5:4) — profaning objects set apart for God's worship for the sake of pagan revelry, the night the kingdom fell.

Persian administrators maneuver King Darius into signing an irrevocable decree: for thirty days, no one may petition any god or man except the king himself, on pain of being thrown into a den of lions (Daniel 6:7–9). Unlike Babylon's image, this law targets not a single ceremonial act but ongoing devotional practice — it makes prayer itself, addressed to anyone but the king, a capital offense.

Which commandment it breaks: This is a narrower but sharper violation than Babylon's — a direct assault on the first commandment alone, since the law doesn't require worship of an idol, only the exclusion of God as an object of address. Daniel's response (Daniel 6:10) is deliberately public and unchanged in form: he continues praying toward Jerusalem three times a day, window open, exactly as before. The text frames the conflict as being entirely about who receives his petitions — nothing else about his conduct as a Persian administrator is in question.

The device of "the law of the Medes and Persians, which cannot be altered" (Daniel 6:8, 12, 15) is itself notable: an empire builds a legal system around the fiction of an unchangeable human decree, in a story whose entire tension comes from that decree colliding with the one unchangeable law that actually claims that status — God's.

Of the four, the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes (r. 175–164 BC) mounts the broadest assault on the first tablet, and the only one of the four recorded outside Daniel itself, in the intertestamental books of 1 and 2 Maccabees. His campaign to forcibly Hellenize Judea is not a single decree but a sustained policy:

  • Commandments 1 & 2: he desecrates the Second Temple by rededicating it to Olympian Zeus and setting up "the abomination of desolation" — almost certainly a pagan altar or idol — on the sacred altar itself (1 Maccabees 1:54; cf. Daniel 11:31), and orders sacrifice of pigs on it, a direct substitution of pagan worship for the worship of God in God's own house.
  • Commandment 4: he explicitly outlaws Sabbath observance and the traditional feasts under penalty of death (1 Maccabees 1:44–50) — the clearest case among the four empires of a state directly criminalizing the fourth commandment by name, not merely sidelining it.
  • He further orders the burning of Torah scrolls and the execution of anyone found possessing a copy of "the book of the covenant" (1 Maccabees 1:56–57) — an attack not just on an act of worship but on the very text that defines what worship God requires.

This is the episode that provoked the Maccabean Revolt, and it stands in the tradition as the most complete first-tablet assault of the pre-Roman empires — touching worship, idolatry, and Sabbath-keeping all at once, rather than isolating a single commandment as Babylon and Persia each did.

Rome is the odd one out in this list, because it breaks the first tablet twice, from opposite directions, separated by roughly a century and a half and a change of official religion.

Phase one — pagan Rome (Decius, 250 AD; later Diocletian, c. 303 AD): the empire, still openly polytheistic, demands sacrifice to the Roman gods on pain of death — a direct rerun of Babylon's Commandments 1 & 2 violation, complete with its own version of the golden-image ultimatum, this time formalized into empire-wide bureaucratic procedure (the libellus certificate system, detailed below).

Phase two — Christianized Rome (Laodicea, c. 363 AD; Theodosius, 380–392 AD; Charlemagne, 789–800 AD): once the empire itself becomes nominally Christian, the direction of coercion inverts, but on the historicist reading it lands on the same tablet from a different angle. Rather than forcing pagan worship, the state and church together discourage, then forbid, then criminalize the biblical seventh-day Sabbath, replacing it with Sunday observance backed by increasingly severe civil penalty — a slower-motion, more legally layered version of what Antiochus did to Judea by outright decree. The claim isn't that Sunday observance itself is a "graven image"; it's that a human institution was substituted, under civil compulsion, for what this tradition reads as a specific, named requirement of the fourth commandment.

Objection Considered
"Isn't it inconsistent to call pagan Rome and Christian Rome the 'same kingdom' committing the 'same' violation, when their theology is the opposite of each other?"

The claim isn't that the theology is identical — it plainly isn't. The claim is narrower: that the same political entity, understood in Daniel's own scheme as one continuous "fourth kingdom" (the legs and feet of iron in Daniel 2, read by historicists as running unbroken through the Papal era), used the same tool — civil law backed by penalty — against the same target — the first tablet — twice, from opposite theological directions. Continuity of method and continuity of political entity is the argument; continuity of doctrine is explicitly not being claimed.

The next section works through that second phase in the detail it deserves — it's the one with the most surviving primary-source paper trail, four documented steps across four centuries, and it's the one this document's larger series (The Venerable Day) is built around.

Comparative View

Who Resisted, and How

Each episode above produced its own faithful minority — and the shape of their resistance is remarkably consistent: not armed defiance of the state generally, but continuation, in public, of the specific act of worship the decree targeted.

Babylon Bow to the golden image Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego Public refusal to bow; no attempt to hide or flee Delivered from the furnace (Daniel 3:26–27)
Medo-Persia Pray to no god or man but the king Daniel Continued praying openly, in his usual place, at his usual times Delivered from the lions (Daniel 6:22–23)
Greece Eat unclean sacrificial meat; abandon Sabbath and Torah The Maccabee family; Eleazar; the mother and seven sons (2 Maccabees 6–7) Refusal of forced pagan sacrifice, at the cost of torture and death; eventually armed revolt Martyrdom for many; the Maccabean Revolt ultimately restores Jewish worship (commemorated at Hanukkah)

Rome (Christianized) Rest and worship on Sunday; abandon the seventh-day Sabbath Sabbath-keeping minorities across later centuries (variously identified with groups such as the Waldenses and, after the Reformation, emerging Sabbatarian movements) Continued Saturday observance despite social, economic, and at times legal penalty Persistence as a minority position through to the present rather than a single decisive resolution

The Rome (Christianized) row is the least tightly documented of the five — unlike the Maccabean martyrs or the Decian libelli, the historical continuity of specific Sabbath-keeping groups across many centuries is itself debated among historians, and this document doesn't overstate that continuity as a single unbroken chain.

Rome, Phase One · 250 AD

The Edict of Decius

In 250 AD, Emperor Decius issued an empire-wide decree requiring every inhabitant of Rome to perform a public sacrifice to the Roman gods, pour a wine libation, and taste the sacrificial meat — all before a Roman magistrate. It wasn't aimed specifically at Christians; it was a loyalty test following a period of instability, intended to restore pax deorum, the "peace of the gods." But for a monotheistic minority who viewed any participation as idolatry, it functioned as a direct assault on conscience — Babylon's golden image, reissued in bureaucratic form.

The Libellus: Proof of Compliance

Citizens who complied received a signed certificate — a libellus — as legal proof they had sacrificed. Because Rome recorded permanent law in stone or bronze but everyday bureaucracy on papyrus, these certificates only survive where the climate is exceptionally dry. Nearly all known examples were recovered from Egypt, particularly the town rubbish heaps of Oxyrhynchus and Theadelphia.

"I have always sacrificed to the gods, and now in your presence I have poured a libation, sacrificed, and tasted the meat."

Formula recorded on surviving libelli, 250 AD

What survives

A 22-line Greek libellus belonging to Aurelia Charis of Theadelphia (Berlin Neues Museum, with a duplicate in Hamburg); the Libellus of Aurelia Bellias, dated 21 June 250 AD (University of Michigan Library); and P.Oxy. XII 1464, dated 27 June 250 AD, held among the Oxyrhynchus Papyri at Oxford's Sackler/Bodleian Libraries.

The countersignature

Roman magistrates then signed in a different hand, witnessing the act — typically some version of "We, the commissioners, witnessed you sacrificing."

Refusal carried real consequences — imprisonment, torture, or execution — which is why the Decian persecution produced both martyrs and a large number of lapsi, Christians who complied (or bought forged certificates) to survive, a controversy that occupied the church for a generation afterward.

Rome, Phase Two, Step One · c. 363–364 AD

Council of Laodicea, Canon 29

A little over a century after Decius, the direction of coercion begins to invert. A synod of bishops meeting in Laodicea (Asia Minor) issued a set of disciplinary canons aimed at drawing a firmer line between Christian and Jewish practice. Canon 29 addressed the Sabbath directly — the first documented instance in this sequence of Rome's first-tablet violation shifting from Commandments 1–2 (pagan sacrifice) toward Commandment 4 (the Sabbath):

"Christians must not judaize by resting on the Sabbath, but must work on that day, rather honouring the Lord's Day; and, if they can, resting then as Christians. But if any shall be found to be judaizers, let them be anathema from Christ."

Council of Laodicea, Canon 29 (trans., New Advent Church Fathers Archive)

Read plainly, the canon does three things: it forbids Saturday rest as "judaizing," commends Sunday observance instead, and threatens excommunication (anathema) for noncompliance. It sat alongside other anti-Judaizing canons from the same council — Canon 37 forbidding Christians from receiving festival gifts from Jews, and Canon 38 forbidding the eating of unleavened bread during Passover — indicating the council's broader concern was separating Christian identity from Jewish practice, not sabbath theology as an isolated question.

Historical context worth holding onto

Laodicea was a regional council, not an ecumenical one binding the whole church, and it had no civil enforcement power of its own. Its significance here is that it shows church leadership, well before any Roman emperor legislated the matter, already treating Sabbath-keeping as something to be actively discouraged — church discipline preparing the ground that civil law would later occupy.

Rome, Phase Two, Step Two · 380–392 AD

Theodosius I and the Edict of Thessalonica

A little over a century after Decius forced pagan worship on everyone, the direction of state coercion reversed entirely. Theodosius I (r. 379–395) made Nicene Christianity the empire's official religion and, through a series of decrees between 389 and 392 AD, criminalized the paganism Decius had once enforced.

380 AD
Edict of Thessalonica

Declared Nicene Christianity the empire's official state religion, explicitly naming the Bishops of Rome and Alexandria as the standard of correct doctrine. Non-Nicene Christians — chiefly Arians, who held Christ to be a created, subordinate being — were branded "foolish madmen" holding "infamous heresies" and made legally liable to punishment.

389–392 AD
The Theodosian decrees

Criminalized animal sacrifice, divination, and both public and private pagan worship — including household offerings of wine or incense to the Lares and Penates. Authorized the closure and demolition of pagan temples across the empire, and is widely credited with ending the ancient Olympic Games (c. 393 AD) on account of their pagan origin.

Church and state entwined

Theodosius went further than Constantine, who had merely legalized Christianity in 313 AD. He wove it into imperial law itself: bishops received state insignia and were increasingly given civil roles as judges and administrators. But the union cut both ways. After Theodosius ordered a massacre of roughly 7,000 citizens in Thessalonica in 390 AD following a riot, Bishop Ambrose of Milan barred him from the cathedral and refused him communion until he performed months of public penance — establishing, for the first time, that even the emperor was answerable to ecclesiastical authority. This is the moment the "church-state alliance" pattern this document traces becomes formal and mutual, rather than one side simply dominating the other.

Rome, Phase Two, Step Three · c. 789–800 AD

Charlemagne's Sunday Laws

Four centuries after Laodicea, Charlemagne, King of the Franks, took what had been church discipline and turned it into binding civil law across the territories he ruled. He was already a baptized Christian; Pope Leo III did not convert him. What happened on Christmas Day, 800 AD, in Old St. Peter's Basilica was a coronation — Leo crowned him "Emperor of the Romans," reviving the title of the fallen Western Roman Empire and effectively founding what became the Holy Roman Empire.

The relationship behind that coronation was transactional as much as spiritual: Leo III had been attacked by political rivals in Rome and needed Charlemagne's military protection; Charlemagne, in turn, gained religious legitimacy for his rule. Einhard, Charlemagne's biographer, records that the king claimed the coronation caught him by surprise — historians still debate whether that was genuine or political theater.

The Admonitio Generalis and beyond

Through capitularies such as the Admonitio Generalis (789 AD), Charlemagne mandated not just a day of rest but compulsory church attendance, going further than Constantine's original 321 AD civil rest day — and completing, in effect, the same journey Antiochus IV made by decree in a single stroke centuries earlier in Judea, except spread across four centuries and several distinct legal instruments:

Required

Attendance at Mass. Public markets, court cases, and business dealings were banned on Sundays so nothing would compete with it.

Forbidden work (by law)

For men: plowing, harvesting hay, felling trees, building, gardening. For women: sewing, weaving, washing clothes — spelled out in granular detail.

Enforcement varied sharply by population. Frankish subjects faced fines or public shaming. In newly conquered, still-pagan Saxon territory, the Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae made refusal of baptism or failure to observe Christian holy days a capital offense — civil penalty for a first-tablet matter, the same structural move as Antiochus's decree in 1 Maccabees, now enforced by a self-identified Christian emperor instead of a pagan one.

Word Study

"Son of Perdition": Judas and the Antichrist

The exact phrase "son of perdition" (Greek ho huios tēs apōleias) occurs only twice in the New Testament: once applied to Judas Iscariot (John 17:12) and once to the end-time "man of lawlessness" (2 Thessalonians 2:3).

The parallel

  • Insider status — Judas was not an outsider to Christ's ministry; he was one of the twelve, present at healings and preaching, entrusted with the money bag.
  • Concealment — at the Last Supper, the other disciples didn't suspect him; each asked "Lord, is it I?" (Matthew 26:22).
  • Inward vs. outward reality — Luke 22:3 states "Satan entered Judas," describing a hidden spiritual reality behind an outwardly faithful appearance.

Paul's description in 2 Thessalonians 2:4 places the parallel figure not in a pagan temple but "in the temple of God, showing himself that he is God" — language many commentators (both historicist Protestant and otherwise) take to indicate the deception arises from within professed Christianity rather than as an openly hostile external force. On the linguistic point: the Greek prefix anti- can carry the sense "against" but also "in place of, a substitute for" — which is why "antichrist," in much of the historic Christian tradition (not only Adventist), has been read as "a counterfeit standing in Christ's place" rather than simply "an opponent of Christ."

Symbolism

Mystery Babylon and the Harlot's Daughters

Revelation 17–18 describes a woman named "Mystery, Babylon the Great, the Mother of Harlots" (Rev. 17:5). In the prophetic symbolism used throughout Scripture, a chaste woman typically represents a faithful covenant community, and an unfaithful woman an apostate one (see Jeremiah 6:2, Hosea, Ezekiel 16 and 23). On that convention:

  • The Mother — historicist interpreters, going back through the Reformation, have widely identified this figure with a corrupted, politically empowered church-state system centered on Rome — the same phase-two Rome traced through Laodicea, Theodosius, and Charlemagne above.
  • The Daughters — the "harlot's daughters" are read as bodies that separated from that system at the Reformation but, in this reading, retained inherited traditions not directly rooted in Scripture — Sunday observance being the most frequently cited example, since it traces to Constantine's civil decree and later church councils rather than to a New Testament command.

Revelation 18:7 has the woman boast, "I sit as a queen, and am no widow, and will not see sorrow" — language that closely echoes Isaiah 47:8, where the prophet puts nearly identical words in the mouth of literal ancient Babylon. That verbal echo is one of the strongest textual links historicist readers point to for treating Revelation's "Babylon" as a conscious, deliberate reuse of the Isaiah 47 oracle rather than coincidence.

Primary Source Check

"A Lock of Her Hair" — The O'Brien Quote, Verified

This quote circulates widely in Adventist and other Sabbatarian literature, so it's worth sourcing precisely. It comes from Fr. John A. O'Brien, a Catholic priest and theologian, in his book The Faith of Millions (first published 1938, revised 1956):

"That [Sunday] observance remains as a reminder of the Mother Church from which the non-Catholic sects broke away — like a boy running away from home, but still carrying in his pocket a picture of his mother or a lock of her hair."

John A. O'Brien, The Faith of Millions (1956 ed.)

The argument O'Brien is making is a Catholic apologetic one: since Scripture nowhere commands Sunday observance in place of the Sabbath, Protestants who hold "Scripture alone" as their rule of faith are, by his account, unknowingly still deferring to Catholic ecclesiastical tradition every time they keep Sunday. It's used here by Sabbatarian writers to make the same point from the opposite direction — as evidence that the change is acknowledged, by Rome's own writers, to be a matter of church authority rather than biblical command.

Also commonly cited alongside it

The Catechism of Catholic Doctrine (Baltimore Catechism tradition), "Convert's Catechism" editions (e.g. 1910): Q: "Which is the Sabbath day?" A: "Saturday is the Sabbath day." Q: "Why do we observe Sunday instead of Saturday?" A: "We observe Sunday instead of Saturday because the Catholic Church transferred the solemnity from Saturday to Sunday." These catechetical Q&A quotes are genuine and widely reprinted, though it's worth noting Catholic theology generally frames the change as apostolic-era practice honoring the Resurrection, formalized by later church authority — not as evidence the church claims power superior to Scripture, which is the inference Sabbatarian writers draw from it.

Typology

The Euphrates Dries Up: Isaiah 47 and Revelation 16

Ancient Babylon fell to Cyrus the Great in 539 BC by a specific stratagem: Persian engineers diverted the Euphrates, which ran directly through the city and fed its defensive moats, into an upstream basin. The river dropped low enough for Cyrus's troops to march in along the riverbed, beneath the unguarded river gates, during a night of royal feasting (the events behind Daniel 5's "handwriting on the wall").

Revelation 16:12 reuses this image for the sixth plague: "the great river Euphrates... its water was dried up, so that the way of the kings from the east might be prepared." Because Revelation elsewhere defines its own symbols, and Revelation 17:15 explicitly glosses "the waters... where the harlot sits" as "peoples, multitudes, nations, and tongues," historicist readers take the Euphrates in 16:12 the same way: not a literal river, but the mass of popular and political support sustaining the Babylon system. On that reading, its "drying up" pictures the withdrawal of that global support rather than a literal hydrological event — paired with Revelation 17:16, where the same nations that once supported the system turn against it.

Applying the Pattern Today

Reading This Pattern Into the Present

Everything above — the four kingdoms, Rome's two phases, the Babylon typology — is either documented history or a long-standing line of biblical interpretation. What follows is different in kind: it's the step of mapping that pattern onto current institutions and technologies (digital currency, digital ID, AI surveillance). That step deserves to be held more loosely, for a specific reason.

Worth being clear-eyed about

Central bank digital currencies, national digital ID systems, and AI-driven data analysis are real and developing. But whether any of them will be repurposed as the specific enforcement mechanism of a future religious persecution is interpretation of unfolding events, not something that can be verified now — in the way the libelli in a Berlin museum case or the text of Canon 29 can be. Every generation of Sabbath-keeping and historicist writers since at least the 19th century has pointed to the "current" technology or institution of their day (national ID cards, barcodes, the EU, RFID chips, smartphones) as the likely mechanism — and been wrong about the timing each time. That track record doesn't mean the underlying theological pattern is wrong; it does mean confidence about which specific present-day technology fulfills it should be held with real humility, and stated as "how I read current events through this framework" rather than as settled fact when you're writing for others.

Objection Considered
"Isn't finding this pattern in every era just confirmation bias — selecting only the examples that fit and ignoring everything that doesn't?"

Worth taking seriously rather than waving off. The four kingdoms themselves aren't cherry-picked for this document — they're the anchor structure of Daniel's own visions (Daniel 2 and 7), so the base pattern is textual, not imposed. The risk of confirmation bias rises sharply, though, the moment the pattern gets extended past those four named kingdoms to arbitrary modern institutions — that's exactly the move the "Reading This Pattern Into the Present" section above is flagging as interpretation rather than established fact, and it's a fair challenge to keep asking of any specific modern application, including ones made in this document's own related pieces.

What can be said more confidently

  • The historical pattern of state power being used to coerce worship, from Nebuchadnezzar through Charlemagne, is real and well documented — that part of the case doesn't rest on speculation.
  • Modern financial and surveillance infrastructure genuinely does make certain forms of large-scale economic exclusion more technically feasible than in earlier centuries — that's a factual observation about capability, separate from any claim about intent or prophetic timing.
  • Where the case becomes speculative is the leap from "this is now technically possible" to "this is what Revelation is describing and it's imminent." That's a theological judgment call, not a historical or technical fact, and sincere, informed Christians — including within Sabbatarian traditions — differ on how far to press it.

Bringing It Together

Closing Synthesis

Strip away the specific empires and the pattern is simple: Scripture divides the law into duties toward God and duties toward neighbor, and four times in the historical record a state used its full coercive power — not against theft or murder, but against the first category — worship itself. Babylon demanded it; Medo-Persia forbade its rightful object; Greece did both, and outlawed the Sabbath by name; Rome did all of it twice, once as a pagan power compelling sacrifice, and once as a Christianized power compelling a different day of rest in the Sabbath's place.

What stays constant across all four is not the theology of the empire doing the compelling — that varies from openly pagan to explicitly Christian — but the method: civil law reaching into the first tablet, backed by penalties ranging from social exclusion to execution. And what stays constant on the other side is the shape of the response: not rebellion against the state as such, but the quiet, public, unhidden continuation of a specific act of worship, regardless of the cost attached to it.

Whether that pattern is presently repeating in some new form is a question this document has deliberately declined to answer with confidence — the historical chain from Decius to Charlemagne stands on its own primary sources regardless of how that further question is resolved.
