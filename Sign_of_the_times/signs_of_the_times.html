What is a Proxy War?

A proxy war occurs when a major power funds, arms, trains, or directs a smaller force to fight its geopolitical battles — avoiding direct confrontation between superpowers. During the Cold War (1947–1991), the US and USSR fought through proxies in Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Mozambique, Nicaragua, Afghanistan, and dozens more. Today proxy warfare continues in Yemen (Saudi/Iran), Syria (US/Russia/Turkey/Iran), and Ukraine (NATO/Russia). Since 1945, the UCDP counts over 100 proxy conflicts — responsible for 20+ million deaths. Proxy wars are deliberately deniable and often far longer-lasting than conventional wars. The Cold War produced more total deaths through proxies than WWI.

Colonial Wars & State-Manufactured Famine

The Hidden Body Count of Empire

Colonial violence — wars of conquest, deliberate famines, concentration camps, forced labour, and resource extraction backed by military force — killed tens of millions between 1500 and 1960. These deaths are systematically underrepresented in Western historical accounts. Historian Mike Davis's Late Victorian Holocausts (2001) documents that colonial policies converted regional droughts into mass famines killing 12–29 million in British India alone between 1876 and 1902. The death toll of empire rivals — and in some estimates exceeds — the Holocaust.

Similar manufactured famines occurred in the Great Bengal Famine (1943) — when Churchill diverted food from Bengal to European war stocks; 2–3 million died.

Financial Wars & Economic Warfare

When Money Becomes a Weapon

Economic warfare — sanctions, currency manipulation, debt weapons, trade blockades, financial coercion, and market attacks — has grown from a peripheral tool to a primary instrument of geopolitical power. Today, financial warfare can collapse nations without a single bullet.

Definition of Financial War

Financial warfare encompasses: trade embargoes and blockades, currency devaluation attacks, debt trap diplomacy, banking system exclusions (SWIFT), sovereign debt weaponization, petrodollar enforcement, cryptocurrency sanctions evasion, commodity market manipulation, and the use of multinational corporations as geopolitical tools.

Ancient Economic Warfare

Athens' Megarian Decree (432 BC) — a trade embargo excluding Megara from Athenian markets — is considered by Aristophanes to be the economic trigger for the Peloponnesian War. Rome's control of grain supply (annona) was weaponized against political enemies. Julius Caesar cut off tribes from Roman trade to force submission.

Tuberculosis — The Greatest Infectious Killer in Human History

Mycobacterium tuberculosis has been infecting humans for at least 9,000 years (detected in Neolithic skeletons). The eradication of smallpox is arguably the single greatest achievement in the history of medicine — saving an estimated 5 million lives per year since 1980.

The WHO defines 21 Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) — a cluster of parasitic, bacterial, and viral infections that overwhelmingly affect the world's poorest 1.7 billion people across 149 countries. They are "neglected" because they primarily kill and disable people with no purchasing power — making them commercially unattractive for drug development.

Diet

The Seed Oil Revolution — 115 Years of an Uncontrolled Experiment

Before 1900, humans consumed fat primarily from animals (tallow, lard, butter) and traditional plant sources (olive oil, coconut oil, palm oil). These fats have been part of the human diet for hundreds of thousands of years. The invention of hydrogenation (Procter & Gamble, 1911 — Crisco, made from cottonseed oil) introduced the first industrially processed fat to the human food supply. The margarine industry, born from war-time butter shortages, used the same process.

Polyunsaturated seed oils (soybean, canola/rapeseed, sunflower, corn, cottonseed, grapeseed) now constitute ~20% of calories in Western diets — up from near-zero in 1900. They are extraordinarily high in linoleic acid (omega-6), which oxidises when heated, producing 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and other aldehydes — documented cellular toxins. A 2021 study (Tucker et al.) found linoleic acid now constitutes 21% of the fat in adipose tissue samples — up from 9% in 1960, tracking the rise in consumption almost exactly. The hypothesis: oxidised linoleic acid drives mitochondrial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation — the upstream cause of most chronic disease.

Counterpoint: mainstream nutrition science still considers seed oils safe, citing large epidemiological studies showing lower LDL cholesterol. The mechanistic toxicity data and population-level epidemiological data remain in genuine scientific tension — which is itself alarming given the ubiquity of these oils in processed food globally.

Microbiome

The Microbiome Crisis — Destroying the Ecosystem Within

The human gut hosts 38 trillion microbial cells — more than the total number of human cells in the body. This ecosystem (the gut microbiome) trains the immune system, produces neurotransmitters (90% of serotonin), synthesises vitamins (B12, K2, folate), regulates metabolism, and protects against pathogens. It took hundreds of thousands of years to evolve. Modern life is destroying it in a generation.

Key disruptors: Antibiotics — the average American child receives 17 courses of antibiotics by age 18; each broad-spectrum course can reduce gut bacterial species by 25–50%, with some strains never recovering. Caesarean birth (now 32% of US births, 56% in Brazil) deprives the infant of maternal vaginal microbiome seeding — associated with higher rates of asthma, obesity, celiac disease, and allergies. Formula feeding vs. breastfeeding: breast milk contains 200+ prebiotic oligosaccharides specifically designed to feed Bifidobacterium in the infant gut — formula contains none. Glyphosate (active ingredient in Roundup) is a patented antibiotic (US patent 7,771,736) that was originally developed as an industrial descaler; it kills gut bacteria at agricultural residue levels found in food. Ultra-processed food: emulsifiers (polysorbate 80, carboxymethylcellulose) in processed foods shown to disrupt the gut mucus barrier in animal studies.

Consequences of microbiome disruption: obesity, Type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, depression, anxiety, allergies, autoimmune diseases. The gut-brain axis means a disrupted microbiome is a disrupted mind. Faecal microbiota transplant (FMT) — transferring gut bacteria from a healthy donor — has shown remarkable efficacy for C. difficile infection and is being studied for depression, obesity, and autism.

Agriculture

Soil Depletion — Eating Food That Is Eating Itself

The nutritional content of fruits and vegetables has declined significantly since industrial farming replaced traditional agriculture. A landmark 1997 study by Dr. Donald Davis (University of Texas) analysed USDA nutritional data for 43 crops between 1950 and 1999, finding reliable declines in protein, calcium, phosphorus, iron, riboflavin, and vitamin C — average declines of 15–38%. A 2004 update found similar declines. British government data (1940 vs. 1991) showed: broccoli lost 75% of its calcium content; spinach lost 60% of its iron; watercress lost 93% of its copper content.

Causes: "Dilution effect" — modern high-yield varieties grow so fast they cannot absorb nutrients from soil at the same rate as slower-growing heritage varieties. Continuous monoculture farming depletes soil mineral content without adequate replacement. Heavy tilling destroys soil mycorrhizal fungi networks (the "wood wide web" of soil) that make minerals bioavailable to plants. Synthetic nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium (NPK) fertilisers replace these three macronutrients but neglect the 70+ trace minerals crops need and humans require. It is entirely possible to eat a calorically adequate diet today and be severely deficient in magnesium, zinc, selenium, iodine, and chromium — all essential cofactors for metabolic and neurological function.

The implication: people eating the same food their grandparents ate are receiving substantially less nutrition. The response — fortification and supplementation — is a downstream fix that does not address the upstream agricultural crisis. Global topsoil is being lost at 10–40× the rate of natural replenishment; at current rates, the UN estimates 60 years of topsoil remain.

Circadian

The Sleep Crisis — Electric Light and the Hijacked Circadian Rhythm

Edison's incandescent bulb (1879) began the process of decoupling human sleep from the solar cycle for the first time in 300,000 years of human evolution. The circadian rhythm — governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus and regulated by light-sensitive melanopsin cells in the retina — expects darkness after sunset. Electric light, and especially blue-spectrum LED and screen light, suppresses melatonin production and delays sleep onset by 1–3 hours on average.

The data: average US sleep has fallen from 8.9 hours (1942, Gallup) to 6.8 hours (2013, Gallup). 1 in 3 Americans is chronically sleep-deprived (CDC). In South Korea, average sleep is 6 hours 3 minutes — the least in the world. Shift workers (20% of the workforce in developed nations) have dramatically elevated rates of cancer, heart disease, diabetes, and obesity — the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies shift work as a probable carcinogen (Group 2A).

Matthew Walker's research (Why We Sleep, 2017) synthesised decades of sleep science: every tissue in the body is damaged by insufficient sleep. The immune system: one night of 4-hour sleep reduces natural killer cell activity by 70%. Cancer: men sleeping 6 hours or fewer have a 40% higher prostate cancer risk. Dementia: the glymphatic system that clears amyloid-beta (Alzheimer's precursor) from the brain operates almost exclusively during deep sleep. The cardiovascular system: the one-hour sleep loss from daylight saving time is associated with a 24% increase in heart attacks the following day. Yet modern life systematically penalises adequate sleep — glorifying busyness, rewarding early rising, and filling evenings with screens.
