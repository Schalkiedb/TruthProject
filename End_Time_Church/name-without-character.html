The Name Without the Nature

How Scripture Warns That Most of the Damned Will Be Professing Christians

The most sobering thread in prophetic Scripture is not the wickedness of pagans — it is the multitude who carry the name of Christ while having no covenant with His character. From Isaiah's ancient oracle to Christ's own words at the Sermon on the Mount, the Bible draws a consistent portrait: in the last days, the broadest road to destruction will be paved with religious profession.

Before studying the passages, understand the symbols Scripture uses. The Bible is its own interpreter.

The Name Without the Yoke — Wanting Christ's Identity, Refusing His Cross

Perhaps no image captures modern nominal Christianity more sharply than Isaiah 4:1 — a mass clamour for the Bridegroom's name while every covenant condition is stripped away.

Isaiah 4:1
"Only let us be called by your name"
+
"And in that day seven women shall take hold of one man, saying, We will eat our own bread, and wear our own apparel: only let us be called by thy name; take away our reproach!" — Isaiah 4:1, KJV
⚠ How to Read This Verse — Prophetic Symbols Defined by Scripture This verse is prophetic imagery. The Bible interprets its own symbols:

Woman = Church / God's covenant people. God addresses His people as a woman throughout the prophets: "I have likened the daughter of Zion to a comely and delicate woman" (Jeremiah 6:2). Paul calls the Church a "chaste virgin" betrothed to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2). The marriage covenant between God and His people is the central metaphor of Scripture (Ephesians 5:25–32).

Man / Bridegroom = Christ. God declared Himself Israel's husband (Isaiah 54:5). John the Baptist identified Christ as the Bridegroom (John 3:29). Revelation 19:7 anticipates the "marriage of the Lamb."

Bread = The Word of God / sound doctrine. "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4).

Apparel / Clothing = Righteousness / character. Revelation 19:8 explicitly defines it: "the fine linen is the righteousness of saints." The Laodicean church is told it is spiritually "naked" and must buy "white raiment" from Christ (Revelation 3:17–18).

With the symbols decoded: seven churches woman = church press urgently upon Christ man = Christ — not in genuine covenant, but on the most hollow terms imaginable. They explicitly waive two covenant obligations:

"We will eat our own bread" bread = doctrine — they will sustain themselves spiritually on their own theology, their own tradition, their own interpretation. They refuse Christ's provision of truth. This is the congregation that shapes doctrine to personal preference (2 Timothy 4:3–4).

"We will wear our own apparel" clothing = righteousness — they will clothe themselves in their own righteousness rather than the robe of Christ's character. This is the man who arrives at the wedding feast "not having a wedding garment" (Matthew 22:11) — he sought the celebration but refused the Bridegroom's terms.

Yet they ask for one thing: "Only let us be called by thy name." The name is everything; the covenant is nothing. They want the badge of Christianity without its transformation. The reproach they seek to remove is the social shame of being without a Saviour — not the conviction of sin. They want cover, not conversion.

The early church father Victorinus of Poetovio (c. AD 260) interpreted this verse with exactly this prophetic lens — the seven women as the seven churches of Revelation and the one man as Christ: they were "receiving His bread, and clothed with His apparel, who ask that their reproach should be taken away, only that His name should be called upon them." What Victorinus saw: churches that take the benefits while disclaiming the obligations.

The Nominal Confession in Plain Language "We will devise our own doctrine (our own bread). We will generate our own righteousness (our own clothing). Only — only — call us Christian. Remove the reproach of being without a Saviour. But impose no yoke. Demand no obedience. Change nothing in us."

David Wilkerson called this portrait "the church of forgiveness only" — a faith that accepts pardon but not transformation, that wants the Bridegroom's name on the door but not His lordship in the house. Isaiah 4:1 is the prophetic archetype of every nominal, consumer-Christianity congregation in human history.

This may be the most terrifying passage in the New Testament — not because it describes pagans, but because it describes ministers. The condemned are not casual unbelievers; they are active religious workers who prophesied, cast out demons, and performed mighty works — all in Christ's name.

πολλοί polloi G4183

Meaning: "Many" — nominative masculine plural of polus. The same root that gives English "poly-" (many). Its antonym is ὀλίγοι oligoi — "few" (Matthew 7:14). Together they form the asymmetric verdict of the Sermon on the Mount.

Jesus uses polloi twice in immediate sequence — "many there be" on the road to destruction (v.13) and "polloi will say to me in that day" (v.22). This is not rhetorical hyperbole. The same word is used in Romans 5:15 for the vast mass of humanity. Christ is saying that the multitude arriving at judgment bearing His name will be turned away — not a minority exception, but the majority of self-identified Christians.

Οὐδέποτε ἔγνων Oudepote egnōn G3763 + G1097

"I never knew you" — two words that dismantle a lifetime of religious activity. Oudepote (G3763) is a compound absolute negative: oude (not even) + pote (at any time). It means "not even once, at any point in time, ever." This is not a relationship that cooled; it is a relationship that never began.

The verb is γινώσκω ginōskō (G1097) — not oida (factual, cognitive knowledge) but the deep, experiential, relational knowing of intimate partnership. Ginōskō is the same word used in Matthew 1:25 for Joseph "knowing" Mary — the most intimate union possible. It describes the Good Shepherd who "knows" His sheep (John 10:14–15), the Father who "knows" the Son. Christ is not saying "I was unaware of you." He is saying: there was never any relational union between us, regardless of what you did in My name. The name was used; the Person was never received.

τὴν ἀνομίαν tēn anomian G458

"Workers of iniquity" — the KJV softens this. The Greek is anomia: a- (without) + nomos (law). Literally: lawlessness. They are not "wicked" in some vague moral sense — they are people who lived without the law of God as their governing principle. Note the definite article: tēn anomian — "the lawlessness," pointing to a specific, defined condition Christ has diagnosed.

This directly links Matthew 7:23 to the Sabbath question and the law of God. The New Living Translation renders it plainly: "you who break God's laws." The connection to Isaiah 4:1 is exact: those women refused His apparel (righteousness) and His bread (doctrine/law) — and here Christ confirms that the rejected multitude were, beneath their prophesying, practitioners of lawlessness.

The connecting thread to Isaiah 4:1 is unmistakable: these people also wanted the name — "in thy name… in thy name… in thy name" (three times, each invoking His authority as a badge). But οὐδέποτε ἔγνων — the Bridegroom never ginōskō-knew them. The name was taken; the intimacy was never formed.

The Narrow Way and the Many — The Gate Most Christians Are Not Entering

Immediately before His warning about false professors, Christ describes a road that the majority of religious people are not travelling.

Matthew 7:13–14
Many on the broad way — few finding life
+
"Enter ye in at the strait gate: for wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat: Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it." — Matthew 7:13–14, KJV

The Sermon on the Mount is addressed to disciples — to those already following Jesus. This is not a warning to the openly pagan world. The broad road's multitude includes the religious crowd of verses 21–23: those who will say "Lord, Lord" and be turned away. They chose the wide gate because the narrow one demanded transformation, not merely confession.

στενή / τεθλιμμένη stenē / tethlimmenē G4728 / G2346

Two different Greek words are both translated "narrow" in the KJV, but they describe entirely different qualities. Stenē (G4728) describes the gate — physically narrow, tight, admitting only one person at a time. There is no "family plan" for salvation. Tethlimmenē (G2346, the perfect passive participle of thlibō) describes the way/road — and its meaning is far more severe.

Thlibō means to press, compress, crowd hard upon — to afflict, to cause tribulation. It is the root of θλῖψις thlipsis, the standard New Testament word for tribulation and suffering (Romans 8:35; Revelation 7:14). The KJV's "narrow" is technically correct but anaemic. The ESV renders it "hard." A fuller translation: "the road that leads to life is compressed — pressed in on every side, afflicted, tribulated." Jesus was not merely saying the road is geometrically narrow. He was saying: this road costs you something. It presses the life out of what was worldly in you.

πολλοί · ὀλίγοι polloi · oligoi G4183 · G3641

The Sermon's two quantifiers stand in stark, deliberate contrast. Polloi (G4183) — many, the masses, a great multitude — are on the road to destruction. Oligoi (G3641) — few, a small number — find life.

Jerome (4th century) observed: "Attend to the words, for they have especial force — many walk in the broad way, few find the narrow way. For the broad way needs no search and is not found, but presents itself readily; it is the way of all who go astray." The asymmetry is intentional and unambiguous: Christ's own arithmetic places the overwhelming majority of religious humanity on the road to destruction. The question every reader must face is not whether this is comfortable, but whether it is true — and whether they themselves are among the oligoi or the polloi.

The Pattern of the Sermon Matthew 7 moves as one unit: enter the stenē gate (v.13–14) → beware false prophets who teach the broad way (v.15–20) → the polloi who profess but do not obey are rejected (v.21–23) → those who hear but do not do are the house on sand (v.24–27). The "many" on the broad road are the same "many" who are turned away.

ἀγωνίζεσθε agōnizesthe G75

"Strive" (KJV) — the Greek is agōnizomai, from which we derive "agonize." It is the word for an athlete straining in the games, a soldier fighting in battle. It carries the sense of maximum, concentrated, sustained effort.

When someone asked Jesus whether few would be saved, He did not give a theological answer — He issued an athletic command: agonize to enter. The present tense imperative means: keep striving, keep pressing, do not stop. The same word is used in 1 Corinthians 9:25 for an athlete "competing in the games" and in 1 Timothy 6:12 for "fighting the good fight of faith." The broad way requires no effort — it simply presents itself. The narrow road must be agonized toward. This destroys the comfort of effortless, cost-free Christianity.

When asked directly whether few would be saved, Jesus does not answer the philosophical question — He issues a command: agōnizesthe — agonize to enter. Then He describes those who seek entry but cannot obtain it. Their credential? "We ate and drank in thy presence." They sat in church. They heard His teaching. They participated in religious life. They were, by every social measure, Christians.

His answer echoes Matthew 7:23: "I know you not." The verb here is again γινώσκω ginōskō — relational, experiential knowing, not mere awareness. The relational knowledge required for salvation is not familiarity with Christian culture but a transformed life lived in genuine covenant with Christ.

The Laodicean Church — Christ Outside His Own House

Revelation's most fearful portrait is not of a persecuted church or an ignorant church but of a prosperous, self-satisfied church that has evicted its own Saviour.

Revelation 3:14–20
Lukewarm — neither cold nor hot
+
"I know thy works, that thou art neither cold nor hot: I would thou wert cold or hot. So then because thou art lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I will spue thee out of my mouth. Because thou sayest, I am rich, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked... Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him." — Revelation 3:15–17, 20, KJV

Laodicea is widely understood as the prophetic portrait of the end-time church. It is the seventh and final letter, the only church that receives zero commendation from Christ. There is no "I know your works of love" or "you have kept My word." There is nothing.

Christ Outside the Church The most chilling detail is geographical: Christ is outside. He is knocking on the door of a building that bears His name. The church is full — of programs, of wealth, of self-assessment — but it is empty of Him. Many commentators note there is no evidence of a single born-again believer in the membership; Christ addresses individuals within it, calling anyone who will hear to open the door personally.

The Laodicean condition is not cold atheism. It is warm religiosity — tepid enough to feel comfortable, too tepid to sustain life. This is not the openly wicked person. This is the churchgoer who attends, gives, serves, and goes to hell. Their self-assessment ("I am rich, I have need of nothing") makes their condition more dangerous than open sin, because they see no need to repent. Note that Christ's diagnosis that they are spiritually "naked" naked = no righteousness uses the same clothing symbolism as Isaiah 4:1: they, too, rely on their own apparel.

A Form of Godliness — The Shell Without the Life

Paul's prophetic description of the last days is not a portrait of the world. It is a portrait of the church world.

2 Timothy 3:1–5
Having a form of godliness, but denying its power
+
"This know also, that in the last days perilous times shall come. For men shall be lovers of their own selves, covetous, boasters, proud, blasphemers, disobedient to parents, unthankful, unholy, without natural affection... lovers of pleasures more than lovers of God; having a form of godliness, but denying the power thereof: from such turn away." — 2 Timothy 3:1–2, 4–5, KJV

Paul's nineteen-count indictment of last-days humanity would be frightening enough if he applied it to atheists. But the decisive blow falls in verse 5: these people have a form of godliness. They are, by any external measure, religious. They attend services. They may quote Scripture. They identify as Christian. The Holy Spirit drives home the point with a single phrase: denying its power.

What "Denying the Power" Means The power of godliness is the transforming work of the Holy Spirit — the change of heart, the crucifixion of self, the fruit of the Spirit manifest in daily life. To deny this power is not necessarily to deny it theologically; it is to live as though it is not operating. The form remains; the inner reality is absent. The shell is kept polished. The life has departed.

Paul's instruction is startling: "from such turn away." Not engage. Not reform. Turn away. The implication is that these people will be found within the visible Christian community — so embedded that you must actively choose to separate from their influence.

Tares Among the Wheat — Counterfeits Indistinguishable Until Harvest

Christ's parable reveals the end-time church as a field deliberately seeded with impostors who look identical to the genuine article — until the final judgment separates them.

Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43
The tares are gathered first — bundled for burning
+
"The kingdom of heaven is likened unto a man which sowed good seed in his field: But while men slept, his enemy came and sowed tares among the wheat... The field is the world; the good seed are the children of the kingdom; but the tares are the children of the wicked one... As therefore the tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world." — Matthew 13:24–25, 38, 40, KJV

The tares (zizania) of first-century Palestine were darnel — a weed that is biologically indistinguishable from wheat at every stage of growth except full maturity. This is not a parable about obvious sinners sitting in church. It is about counterfeits so convincing that even angels are instructed not to try to separate them prematurely.

Where Are the Tares? "The field is the world" — but the tares are among the wheat, meaning within the visible community of believers. They attend the same church, sing the same hymns, perhaps even serve in the same ministries. Only at harvest — the close of probation and the judgment — is the difference made visible. The tares are not surprised pagans. They are surprised church members.

The sobering logic: if tares cannot be distinguished from wheat by casual inspection, no one should be confident of their standing based solely on the fact that they are in the field. The question is whether transformation has occurred — whether the grain of genuine repentance and obedience has formed within.

The Great Falling Away — Apostasy From Within

Paul, Peter, and Jude all anticipate a massive defection from genuine faith originating not from outside the church, but from within its own ranks.

2 Thessalonians 2:3
The falling away must come first
+
"Let no man deceive you by any means: for that day shall not come, except there come a falling away first, and that man of sin be revealed, the son of perdition." — 2 Thessalonians 2:3, KJV

The Greek is apostasia — a deliberate departure from truth previously held. This is not the world growing more sinful. The world has always been sinful. This is the church drifting — knowingly moving away from the faith once delivered. Paul calls it a sign that must precede Christ's return. We are living in its fulfilment.

2 Thessalonians 2 Apostasy Great Falling Away
1 Timothy 4:1
Departing from the faith — doctrines of demons
+
"Now the Spirit speaketh expressly, that in the latter times some shall depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, and doctrines of devils." — 1 Timothy 4:1, KJV

The Spirit speaks expressly — this is a direct prophetic word, not a general observation. Those who depart were once in the faith. Apostasy is by definition an inside job. And the mechanism is not overt paganism but doctrines that appear spiritual — seducing spirits operating through religious teachers, replacing the law of God with human tradition and the word of God with charismatic experience.

1 Timothy 4:1 Latter Times Seducing Spirits
2 Peter 2:1–3
False teachers among you — many will follow
+
"But there were false prophets also among the people, even as there shall be false teachers among you, who privily shall bring in damnable heresies, even denying the Lord that bought them, and bring upon themselves swift destruction. And many shall follow their pernicious ways; by reason of whom the way of truth shall be evil spoken of." — 2 Peter 2:1–2, KJV

Peter's prophecy contains two devastating details. First: "among you" — these false teachers arise from within the covenant community. Second: "and many shall follow their pernicious ways." It is not a warning that a fringe minority will be led astray. The word many anticipates a majority response. The broad way will be preached from the pulpit, and it will be popular.

Additional Biblical Witnesses

A constellation of further texts reinforces the same prophetic pattern across both Testaments.

Matthew 22:11–14
Many called — few chosen; the wedding garment refused
+
"And when the king came in to see the guests, he saw there a man which had not on a wedding garment: And he saith unto him, Friend, how camest thou in hither not having a wedding garment? And he was speechless. Then said the king to the servants, Bind him hand and foot, and take him away... For many are called, but few are chosen." — Matthew 22:11–14, KJV

This man entered the banquet. He was in the right place at the right time with the right invitation. But he refused the garment provided — the righteousness of Christ (Revelation 19:8 explicitly defines the fine linen as "the righteousness of saints") garment = righteousness that must be received, not merely acknowledged. Like the women of Isaiah 4:1, he wanted the feast without the King's terms. "Many are called, few are chosen" — the called vastly outnumber the chosen, and the dividing line is the garment.

Matthew 22 Wedding Garment Many Called Few Chosen
James 2:14–17, 19
Faith without works — even demons believe
+
"What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? can faith save him?... Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone... Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble." — James 2:14, 17, 19, KJV

James's devastating observation about demons is that they have impeccable theology — correct doctrine about the existence of God — and they tremble. Doctrinal correctness, even accompanied by emotional response, does not equal saving faith. The faith that saves is the faith that works: that restructures life, produces fruit, and submits to the lordship it confesses. Anything less is the same empty form Paul describes in 2 Timothy 3:5.

James 2 Dead Faith Faith and Works
Titus 1:16
Professing God — denying Him in works
+
"They profess that they know God; but in works they deny him, being abominable, and disobedient, and unto every good work reprobate." — Titus 1:16, KJV

Paul compresses the entire problem into a single verse. The two verbs stand in stark contrast: profess (homologousin — they speak it aloud, formally, publicly) and deny (arnountai — they renounce by their actions). The profession is vocal. The denial is lived. The Greek word for "reprobate" (adokimoi) is the same used in 1 Corinthians 9:27 — disqualified, rejected after examination.

Jude 3–4
Certain men crept in — turning grace into licentiousness
+
"Beloved, when I gave all diligence to write unto you of the common salvation, it was needful for me to write unto you, and exhort you that ye should earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints. For there are certain men crept in unawares, who were before of old ordained to this condemnation, ungodly men, turning the grace of our God into lasciviousness, and denying the only Lord God, and our Lord Jesus Christ." — Jude 3–4, KJV

These men are not outside the church throwing stones. They have crept in — they are seated, welcomed, perhaps leading. Their error is theological: they transform grace from a power that liberates from sin into a license to continue in it. This is hyper-grace theology, antinomianism, the "once saved always saved regardless of conduct" school — precisely the message that Isaiah 4:1 predicts: "We want the name, not the obligation."

The Prophetic Pattern at a Glance

Across six centuries of Scripture — from Isaiah's oracle to John's Apocalypse — a single consistent warning echoes: the Name without the Nature leads to destruction.

Isaiah 4:1
Seven women seek the Bridegroom's name while refusing His provision and covenant — the defining image of end-time nominal Christianity.
Matthew 7:13–14
The way to life is narrow; few find it. The many on the broad road include the religious multitude of vv. 21–23.
Matthew 7:21–23
Many active Christian ministers are rejected at the judgment with the verdict: "I never knew you." The name was used; the relationship never existed.
Luke 13:23–27
Those who "ate and drank in His presence" — cultural church participants — are turned away. Entry requires a striving obedience, not mere attendance.
Matthew 13:24–43
Tares grow indistinguishably among the wheat until harvest. Counterfeits are inside the covenant community, not outside it.
Matthew 22:11–14
Many are called; few are chosen. The man at the feast without the wedding garment is expelled — he wanted the celebration, not the King's righteousness.
Matthew 25:1–13
Half of those actively awaiting Christ's return lacked the oil of genuine transformation. The foolish virgins called "Lord, Lord" — and the door was shut.
Revelation 3:14–20
The end-time church at Laodicea is wealthy, self-satisfied, and spiritually empty. Christ stands outside, knocking to be re-admitted.
2 Timothy 3:1–5
Last-days professing Christians bear nineteen marks of moral collapse while retaining a form of godliness — the shell without the life.
2 Timothy 4:3–4
Church members actively construct a religion tailored to their desires — heaping up teachers with itching ears, turning from truth to fable.
2 Peter 2:1–2
False teachers arise from within; many follow their destructive ways. The majority response will be to the broad-way gospel.
Titus 1:16 · James 2:19 · Jude 4
Professing God while denying Him in deeds. Dead faith indistinguishable from demonic belief. Grace weaponised as a license to sin.

The Searching Question

Scripture's alarm is not directed at the world. The world does not claim the name of Christ. This alarm is sounded over those who do — over the multitude who will stand at the last judgment and say, with genuine shock: "Lord, Lord…"

The question Scripture forces upon every believer is not "Am I a Christian?" but "Does He know me?" — and behind that, "Is the oil in my lamp? Is the fruit on my tree? Have I received the wedding garment? Does my life confirm my profession?"

Isaiah 4:1 and Matthew 7:21–23 are mirror passages. Both show the same tragedy: masses who wanted the Bridegroom's name without His nature. Both close with the same silence — reproach unanswered, the door shut, the verdict final.

"Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves."
— 2 Corinthians 13:5, KJV
