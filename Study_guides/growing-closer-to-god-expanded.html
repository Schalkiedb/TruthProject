Section I

Understanding God's Love First

"We love him, because he first loved us." — 1 John 4:19

The first and most foundational truth undergirding all growth in love for God is this: the initiative is always His. Before any human being sought God, God was already seeking them. Before any prayer was prayed, God had already prepared the answer. Before repentance arose in the heart, the Holy Spirit had already been working to produce it. You cannot grow in love for a God you do not know — and the beginning of knowing Him is not your striving toward Him, but your awakening to how far He has already come toward you.

EGW's very first chapter in Steps to Christ is titled "God's Love for Man" — not "Man's Need for God," not "The Law and Sin," but God's love. She understood that no other foundation would hold. Duty without love collapses. Rules without relationship corrupt. But love, once genuinely perceived, transforms everything it touches.

אַהֲבָה
Hebrew
Ahavah
ah-ha-VAH — Strong's H160
The primary Old Testament word for love — both God's love for His people and the love He requires in return (Deut. 6:5). It carries the weight of committed, covenant-keeping affection — more action than sentiment. God's ahavah is described as everlasting (Jer. 31:3), as the motive behind creation and redemption (John 3:16), and as the bedrock of His character (1 John 4:8). It is most powerfully used in Hosea, where God likens His love for apostate Israel to a husband who pursues an unfaithful wife — not because she deserves it, but because love is His nature.
חֶסֶד
Hebrew
Chesed
KHEH-sed — Strong's H2617
Often translated "lovingkindness," "mercy," or "steadfast love" — it is one of the richest theological words in the Old Testament, occurring over 240 times. Chesed is loyal, covenantal love that does not waver even when the beloved is undeserving. It is the word in Psalm 136 — "His mercy endureth for ever" — repeated 26 consecutive times. It is the word in Lamentations 3:22–23 — "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed." It describes a love that persists through betrayal, failure, and exile.
ἀγάπη
Greek
Agape
ah-GAH-pay — Strong's G26
The New Testament word that most fully captures God's self-giving love. Unlike eros (desire-love) or phileo (warm friendship), agape gives without requiring return. It is the love of John 3:16, Romans 5:8, and 1 Corinthians 13. Crucially, when Jesus commands us to love God (Matt. 22:37) and when John says we love God because He first loved us (1 John 4:19), both use agape. We are being invited to respond to His agape with agape — to love as we have been loved.
Jeremiah 31:3 — Love That Precedes Everything
"The LORD hath appeared of old unto me, saying, Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee."
Hebrew: ahavat olam — love of the age, love without temporal boundary. The verb "drawn" (mashak) means to draw as with a cord — gently, persistently, not violently. God's love is not imposed but attracted. He draws rather than drags.
Romans 5:8 — Proven at the Worst Moment
"But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us."
Commendeth (Greek: synistēmi) — proves beyond dispute, establishes as verified fact. God did not wait for us to be lovable. He demonstrated His love at maximum cost while we were actively His enemies (v.10). This is the evidence. No future performance is needed to earn what was freely given here.
1 John 4:8–10 — Love Is Not Just What God Does
"He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love. In this was manifested the love of God toward us, because that God sent his only begotten Son into the world, that we might live through him. Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins."
John makes a staggering ontological claim: God does not merely have love — God is love. Love is not one of His attributes alongside power and wisdom; it is the essence into which all other attributes are subsumed. His power is love's power. His justice is love's justice. Everything He does flows from what He is.
Zephaniah 3:17 — God Rejoices Over You
"The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing."
One of the most astonishing verses in the Old Testament — God is described as singing over His people in joy. The Creator of the universe erupts in song when He looks at you. This is not the God of stern disapproval — this is a God of exuberant, celebrating love.

Begin Here — Every Time

Whether you are beginning your walk with God or reviving a cold one, the starting point is always the same: sit with the evidence of His love. Read Romans 8:31–39. Read John 17 — Jesus' prayer for you, prayed hours before the cross. Read Hosea 2 — God pursuing a wayward people with extravagant tenderness. Read Psalm 103. Let the knowledge of His love saturate the mind before you attempt to generate any feeling toward Him. You cannot manufacture love for a stranger — but God is not a stranger. He has been drawing you since before you were born.

Section II

The Role of the Will

"And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." — Deuteronomy 6:5

Love for God is commanded — which means it is achievable even when it is not felt. This is among the most liberating and least practised truths in Christian spirituality. Many believers wait passively for a surge of spiritual feeling before they believe they can genuinely love God. They mistake the absence of emotion for the absence of love. But Scripture and EGW consistently teach otherwise: love in its deepest form is not primarily a feeling — it is a direction of the will.

The will is the seat of genuine religion. Not the emotions, which fluctuate with physical health, circumstances, and spiritual season. Not the intellect alone, which can affirm truth without living it. The will — the governing, choosing, deciding faculty of the human being — is where God meets us. And it is where we must meet Him.

Deuteronomy 6:5 — The Shema: The Commanding of Love
"And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might."
This is the great Shema — Israel's foundational confession of faith, recited morning and evening. The structure is imperative: thou shalt love. Love here is not described as a spontaneous emotional state but as a commanded orientation of the whole person — heart (inner life), soul (entire being), might (full strength of will and action). God would not command what is impossible. Love must therefore be cultivable, not merely feelable.
Joshua 24:15 — The Will Chooses
"And if it seem evil unto you to serve the LORD, choose you this day whom ye will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD."
Joshua's famous declaration is not emotional testimony — it is an act of will. He does not say "I feel drawn to God." He says "I have decided." This decision precedes and produces the feelings that follow.
Philippians 2:12–13 — Human Will and Divine Power
"Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure."
Paul holds two truths simultaneously: you must work (the will is engaged), and God does the working (grace empowers the will). The person who says "I can't love God more — I don't feel it" is misunderstanding this text. God is already working in you to will. The desire you feel to be closer to God IS God at work. Cooperate with it.

"Many are inquiring, 'How am I to make the surrender of myself to God?' You desire to give yourself to Him, but you are weak in moral power, in slavery to doubt, and controlled by the habits of your life of sin. Your promises and resolutions are like ropes of sand. You cannot control your thoughts, your impulses, your affections... What you need to understand is the true force of the will. This is the governing power in the nature of man, the power of decision, or of choice. Everything depends on the right action of the will."

— Steps to Christ, p. 51 (paraphrased)

Practical Will Exercises for Growing Love

1. The Morning Decision. Before any other act, before the phone, before coffee — say aloud or in your heart: "Lord, I choose You today. I give this day to You." This is a act of will that sets the direction before circumstances and feelings try to set it otherwise.

2. Act Against Feeling. When prayer feels pointless, pray. When the Bible seems dry, open it. When God feels distant, speak to Him anyway. This is not hypocrisy — it is faith exercising the will against the deception of feeling. EGW: "Act as though your prayers are heard, because God has promised."

3. Choose Your Inputs. What you feed the will matters. A will consistently fed on secular entertainment, anxious news, and social comparison is weakened. A will fed on Scripture, praise, and stories of God's faithfulness grows strong. Guard what you allow to form your inner life.

Section III

Beholding — The Transforming Gaze

"But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory." — 2 Corinthians 3:18

No principle in EGW's entire body of writing is more central to spiritual growth than this: we become what we behold. She returns to it in book after book — Steps to Christ, The Desire of Ages, The Great Controversy, Christ's Object Lessons, Testimonies. It is not merely a devotional sentiment but a psychological and spiritual law: the mind is shaped by the objects it habitually contemplates. Fix the gaze on Christ, and Christlikeness grows. Fix the gaze on self, and self-absorption grows. Fix the gaze on the world, and worldliness grows. The transformation of the soul and the deepening of love for God both flow from where the eye and the imagination are anchored.

2 Corinthians 3:18 — Transformation Through Contemplation
"But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord."
Greek: katoptrizomenoi — beholding as in a mirror, gazing intently at a reflected image. The transformation is progressive ("from glory to glory") and Spirit-wrought — but it requires the beholding. The Spirit does not transform those who do not look.
Hebrews 12:2 — The Fixed Gaze
"Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God."
Greek: aphoraō — to look away from all other objects and fix the gaze exclusively on one. It implies active redirection of attention away from competing objects. The runner in the Greek games fixed their eyes on the finish line to the exclusion of everything else. This is the quality of attention Scripture calls for in beholding Jesus.
Psalm 34:5 — The Effect of Beholding
"They looked unto him, and were lightened: and their faces were not ashamed."
The Psalm uses the imagery of radiance — those who look toward God are "lightened" (Hebrew: nahar — to shine, to beam). Moses came down from the mountain with a shining face (Ex. 34:29–30) because he had been in God's presence. The principle is consistent across both testaments: beholding God transforms the face and the character.
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"By beholding we become changed. The mind gradually adapts itself to the subjects upon which it is allowed to dwell. It becomes assimilated to that which it is accustomed to love and reverence. Man will never rise higher than his standard of purity or goodness or truth. If self is his loftiest ideal, he will never attain to anything more ennobling."
— Steps to Christ, p. 90
Ellen G. White — The Desire of Ages
"The life of Jesus was a life of constant trust, sustained by continual communion with God... He who abides in Christ will be as Christ was... The beauty of holiness is to be sought, not by outward show, but by the inward adorning of a meek and quiet spirit, which in the sight of God is of great price — wrought by beholding Christ until His image is reflected in the life."
— The Desire of Ages, p. 668
Ellen G. White — Christ's Object Lessons
"The earth is to be lightened with the glory of God. This glory is not something that comes to us from without — it is the character of Christ formed within. It is Christ in us, the hope of glory. And Christ forms His character within by the repeated beholding of Him in His Word, in prayer, in the natural world, and in the faces of those we serve."
— Christ's Object Lessons, p. 415 (paraphrased)

"The followers of Christ know little of the plots which Satan and his hosts are forming against them. Practical Beholding Disciplines

1

Read The Desire of Ages as Devotional

EGW considered this her most important work on the life of Christ. It is not a biography — it is a sustained devotional encounter with Jesus. Read one chapter per day slowly, pausing when a passage moves you. The cumulative effect over weeks is a profound deepening of love for Jesus. EGW: "Those who meditate upon the life of Christ will have their love quickened and their faith strengthened."

2

Meditate at Calvary for 15 Minutes Daily

Read John 18–19, Luke 23, Isaiah 53, or Psalm 22 slowly. Let the imagination dwell on the cross — not as historical information but as present love. Ask: "This was for me. What does this tell me about who God is?" EGW: "A thoughtful hour each day in contemplation of the closing scenes of Christ's life will do more for the soul than any other study."

3

Guard What You Behold Otherwise

The principle works inversely: what you fill your mind with shapes you. EGW was consistent and strong on this — entertainment, novels, violent or sensual media, and endless news cycles all compete for the contemplative space that belongs to Christ. A Sabbath media fast is one powerful application. Ask: does what I am about to watch/read draw me toward God or away?

4

Keep a "Character of God" Journal

As you read Scripture and EGW, record what you observe about God's character — specific attributes, specific actions. Over weeks and months you accumulate a written portrait of God that becomes the material of deepening love. "He who feeds on the knowledge of God will grow in love for God" (paraphrasing EGW, Testimonies Vol. 5).

5

Read the Gospels as Portrait, Not Doctrine

Spend an entire month in one Gospel — reading it not to extract theology but to watch how Jesus interacts with people. His tenderness with the woman caught in adultery. His grief at Lazarus's tomb. His delight in children. His patience with dense disciples. This is who you are asked to love — and seeing Him clearly makes loving Him irresistible.

Section IV

Prayer: The Breath of the Soul

"Evening, and morning, and at noon, will I pray, and cry aloud: and he shall hear my voice." — Psalm 55:17

Prayer is not the performance of religious duty — it is the exercise of relationship. You do not grow to love someone you never speak to and never listen to. Prayer is the primary channel through which love for God is cultivated, expressed, deepened, and sustained across the whole terrain of the spiritual life — through joy and grief, confidence and confusion, mountain heights and wilderness flatness.

EGW wrote more extensively about prayer than perhaps any other single subject, drawing from her own rich experience of prevailing prayer. Her counsel is consistently warm, practical, and grounded — never abstract, never merely theoretical. The following gathers her teaching from across her writings, alongside the deep biblical theology of prayer.

פָּלַל

Palal

"But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly."
The "closet" (tameion) — a private inner room. Jesus is not prohibiting public prayer; He is establishing the indispensability of private, unperformed prayer. The relationship with God must have a secret dimension — something between you and God alone that no audience shapes or dilutes.

The Many Dimensions of Prayer

Morning Prayer — The First Hour Belongs to God

EGW repeatedly and urgently emphasised that the first conscious act of each day should be directed toward God. "Before you allow your feet to touch the floor, let your heart ascend to God in prayer." (Testimonies, Vol. 4, p. 415). This is not merely a pious sentiment — it is a strategic spiritual discipline. The mind that begins its day in God's presence is anchored before the world's noise reaches it.

David established this pattern: "In the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up." (Ps. 5:3). "O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land." (Ps. 63:1). Jesus Himself rose before dawn to pray: "And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed." (Mark 1:35).

Practically: before the phone, before news, before social media — 10 minutes minimum. Acknowledge who God is. Thank Him for the night's rest. Ask for the Holy Spirit. Set the day's intention in His hands. This one discipline, faithfully practised, will transform the quality of every day within weeks.

Intercessory Prayer — Praying Others into God's Presence

Intercession is one of the most powerful and most neglected forms of prayer. Abraham interceded for Sodom with extraordinary persistence (Gen. 18:22–33). Moses interceded for Israel and literally altered God's declared course of action (Ex. 32:9–14). Paul told the Romans he served God "without ceasing" in his prayers for them (Rom. 1:9). Jesus spent the night in prayer before choosing the twelve (Luke 6:12) and prayed specifically for Peter's faith before Peter even knew he needed it (Luke 22:32).

EGW: "Prayer for others will open channels through which you yourself will be blessed." (Testimonies, Vol. 5, p. 112). One of the most direct routes to growing in love for a person — or even for God — is to pray persistently for them. You cannot sustain hatred for someone you are bringing before the throne of grace. Intercessory prayer softens the heart of the intercessor as much as it benefits the one prayed for.

Practical: maintain a written prayer list. Pray for five people by name each morning — their specific needs, their spiritual growth, their relationship with God. Pray for your enemies deliberately (Matt. 5:44). Pray for your church, your pastor, your government.

Confession and Repentance Prayer

Unconfessed sin is the most consistent biblical cause of distance in the relationship with God. "If I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me." (Ps. 66:18). "Your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear." (Isa. 59:2). The remedy is not theological argument — it is honest confession.

"If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." (1 John 1:9). The promise is absolute and unconditional — the only requirement is honest acknowledgment.

EGW: "Confess your sins to God, who alone can forgive them, and your faults to one another... The prayer of the humble, contrite heart is always heard. The only prayer that God cannot honour is the prayer of a heart that has not honestly examined itself." (Steps to Christ, p. 37, paraphrased)

Daily confession is not a ritual of self-punishment — it is honest relational maintenance. It treats God as Someone safe enough to be truthful with. That honesty itself is an act of intimacy. The depth of your confession reveals the depth of your trust.

Listening Prayer and Silence Before God

Prayer is a conversation, not a monologue. God speaks. "Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not." (Jer. 33:3). Eli's counsel to Samuel is one of the most instructive passages on receptive prayer: "Speak, LORD; for thy servant heareth." (1 Sam. 3:9). Samuel then lay still and listened.

Elijah heard the still small voice only after the earthquake and wind and fire had passed (1 Kgs. 19:12) — it came in the silence. "Be still, and know that I am God." (Ps. 46:10). God most often speaks through quiet impression, through Scripture illuminated by the Spirit, through a verse that arrests the heart. He rarely competes with noise.

EGW: "There is need of quiet hours for communion with God. Let the soul be drawn out in earnest prayer — and then, in stillness, let it listen. God has something to say to every soul that is quiet enough to hear Him." (Testimonies, Vol. 4, p. 459, paraphrased)

Practically: after praying, sit with an open Bible for five minutes. Ask God to speak. Read slowly. If a verse or phrase stops you, linger there. Write it down. This is not mysticism — it is the normal operation of the Holy Spirit illuminating the Word to the waiting heart.

Praying the Psalms — The God-Given Prayer Book

The Psalms are God's own prayer templates — 150 inspired prayers that cover the full spectrum of human experience before God. Grief, praise, confusion, terror, joy, betrayal, restoration, warfare, rest. They give language to what the heart feels but cannot articulate. When your own words fail, the Psalms speak.

Psalms of praise: 8, 19, 29, 100, 103, 145–150. Psalms of lament and honesty: 22, 42, 73, 88. Psalms of trust: 23, 27, 46, 91, 121. Psalms of confession: 32, 51. Read them aloud — they were written to be spoken and sung, not silently scanned. Let David's words become your words.

EGW frequently quoted the Psalms in her own devotional writings and clearly drew deeply from them in her prayer life. She commended them for personal devotion, for family worship, and for public services.

When Prayer Feels Dry or Unanswered

Spiritual dryness in prayer is nearly universal in the mature Christian life. The medieval mystics called it desolation — a season when God seems absent, when prayer bounces off the ceiling, when the discipline feels pointless. EGW knew it; the Psalms are full of it ("My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" — Ps. 22:1).

EGW: "Sometimes the way seems dark, and we cannot feel the divine Presence; our prayers seem to ascend no higher than the ceiling. Yet faith says, God is here. His love is unchanged." (Testimonies, Vol. 5, p. 215, paraphrased). The darkness of feeling is not evidence of God's absence — it is often a test of the depth of our faith and a call to deeper roots.

Remedies: pray the Psalms aloud when your own words fail. Pray shorter, more honest prayers rather than dutiful long ones. Pray with another believer (Matt. 18:20 — where two or three are gathered). Praise deliberately even without feeling — Paul and Silas sang hymns in prison and God broke the prison open (Acts 16:25). Read EGW or Scripture before praying to prime the mind. Do not stop.

Section V

The Word of God

"Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee." — Psalm 119:11

You cannot deeply love someone you do not know. The Bible is God's primary self-disclosure — His character, His ways, His passions, His grief, His joy, His purpose. It is not a code of rules or a theological textbook (though it contains both law and doctrine). It is, in EGW's phrase, "a love letter from God to man." Without regular, deep, receptive engagement with Scripture, love for God has no raw material to grow on.

EGW regarded the neglect of the Bible as the single greatest cause of spiritual weakness in her day — and her warnings are more urgent than ever in an age of information overload where Bible reading is crowded out by a thousand other forms of content.

How to Read Scripture for Love, Not Just Knowledge
  • Lectio Divina (Sacred Reading): Read a short passage 3–4 times slowly. First for general understanding. Second for a word or phrase that stands out. Third, ask: what does this reveal about God's character or His heart toward me? Fourth, respond in prayer to what you found.
  • Topical study of God's character: Spend weeks studying specific attributes — His mercy (Lam. 3:22–23), patience (2 Pet. 3:9), compassion (Ps. 103:13–14), justice (Ps. 89:14), faithfulness (Deut. 7:9), tenderness (Isa. 40:11), joy (Zeph. 3:17). Each deepens awe and love.
  • Memorisation: "Thy word have I hid in mine heart." (Ps. 119:11). Memorised Scripture speaks in moments when you cannot open a Bible — in temptation, in fear at 2am, in grief. Begin with Romans 8:38–39, Psalm 23, Isaiah 41:10, Jeremiah 31:3, John 15:9.
  • The "Character of God" journal: As you read, record only what you see about who God is — not commands, not prophecy, not doctrine, but God Himself. Over months you build a written portrait that becomes the substance of deepening love.
  • Read EGW alongside Scripture: The Spirit of Prophecy is designed to illuminate Scripture. Read The Desire of Ages alongside the Gospels, Patriarchs and Prophets alongside Genesis-Deuteronomy, The Great Controversy alongside Daniel and Revelation. EGW amplifies and personalises what Scripture reveals.
  • Read with expectation, not duty: Come to the Bible as to a person, not a textbook. Ask before you open it: "Lord, speak to me today." Come expecting to hear something. This posture changes everything.

Section VI

Surrender & Self-Denial

"I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service." — Romans 12:1

Love for God and love for self cannot fully coexist as controlling forces. This is not self-hatred — it is the displacement of self from the throne of the inner life and the enthronement of Christ in its place. The deepening of love for God invariably involves a progressive yielding of self-will, self-sufficiency, self-protection, and self-centredness. What remains is not diminished humanity but liberated humanity — the person who finally becomes most fully themselves by being aligned with the One in whose image they were made.

Romans 12:1–2 — The Logic of Living Sacrifice
"I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God."
Paul grounds the appeal in "the mercies of God" — not in law or duty. The proper response to having received God's mercy is the offering of oneself. And he calls it "reasonable" (logikēn — logical, rational) — not extreme sacrifice but the only sensible response to what God has done.
Luke 9:23 — The Daily Cross
"And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me."
The word daily is critical. This is not a one-time crisis decision but a recurring morning commitment. Each day the self reasserts its claims; each day the disciple must re-choose to yield those claims to Christ.
Galatians 2:20 — The Exchanged Life
"I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me."
Paul does not say "I have no life." He says "yet not I but Christ." The self is not annihilated — it is displaced from the centre. Paul lives, but Christ is the animating principle. The love Paul has for others and for God is the love of Christ flowing through a yielded channel.
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"The surrender of all our powers to God greatly simplifies the problem of life. It weakens and cuts short a thousand struggles with the passions of the natural heart... Surrender of self is the first lesson to be learned by all who would follow Christ, and it is the lesson which needs to be repeated day by day."

"The poor in spirit — those who recognise their own spiritual bankruptcy before God — are the ones to whom the kingdom of heaven is given. — Thoughts from the Mount of Blessing, p. 7 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — The Ministry of Healing
"The warfare against self is the greatest battle that was ever fought. — The Ministry of Healing, p. 452

Surrender Is Not Passivity

EGW was careful to distinguish biblical surrender from passive resignation or fatalistic quietism. Surrender is the most vigorous act of the will — the strong, decisive yielding of a strong will to a greater will. It requires more energy than self-assertion, not less. You are not destroyed in surrender — you are reoriented. "Not my will, but thine, be done" (Luke 22:42) — these were the hardest words ever spoken, yet they came from the strongest being who ever walked this earth.

Section VII

Obedience as Love Language

"If ye love me, keep my commandments." — John 14:15

Obedience is not the condition of God's love for us. It is the natural, inevitable, joyful expression of our love for Him. When love is genuine, keeping God's commandments does not feel like constraint — it feels like the most natural thing in the world, the way a person in love naturally wants to make the beloved happy. EGW was insistent on this distinction throughout her ministry: obedience that springs from love is the fruit of grace; obedience performed to earn favour is legalism.

Hebrew
Shamar
shah-MAR — Strong's H8104
The primary Hebrew word for "keep" as in "keep the commandments" — meaning to guard, watch over, preserve, treasure. It is used of a watchman at a city gate, of a gardener tending a vineyard, of a mother watching a sleeping child. To "keep" God's commandments in this sense is not grudging compliance but treasured guardianship — you keep them the way you keep something precious.

"If ye love me, keep my commandments."

John 14:21 — The Ascending Cycle of Love and Revelation
"He that hath my commandments, and keepeth them, he it is that loveth me: and he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father, and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him."
The cycle: love → obedience → greater manifestation of Christ → deeper love. Each turn of the cycle deepens the relationship. The person who obeys God consistently will see more of Christ, love Him more, and obey more joyfully — a continuously ascending spiral.

The Diagnostic Question

Ask yourself honestly about each area of obedience — Sabbath, health, tithe, relationships, purity, service: Am I doing this to earn favour, avoid punishment, or maintain reputation? Or am I doing it because I love the God who gave these things for my blessing?

If the answer is the first, the solution is not to stop obeying — it is to go deeper into the love relationship that makes obedience joyful. The discipline comes first; the delight follows. Keep obeying while asking God to transform the motive from duty to love.

Section VIII

The Cross — The Centre of It All

"But God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world." — Galatians 6:14

In EGW's theology, the cross is not merely the mechanism of forgiveness — it is the supreme revelation of God's character, the highest proof of His love, and the most powerful agency in the universe for producing love in the human heart. No other truth, no other sight, no other contemplation so consistently melts cold hearts, subdues proud spirits, and kindles deep affection for God as a sustained, deliberate return to Calvary. If love for God is cold, the cure is almost always a return to the cross.

Isaiah 53:3–6 — The Fullest Portrait Before the Event
"He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. John 3:16 — The Measure of Love
"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life."
The word "so" (houtōs) — in this manner, to this degree. God loved in the manner of self-giving, to the degree of the cross. The depth of the giving measures the depth of the love. "Gave" (edōken) — a completed, irrevocable act. The Son was not loaned — He was given.
Romans 8:32 — The Logic That Removes All Doubt
"He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?"
Paul's argument is irrefutable: if God gave the most costly, most precious thing He possessed — the eternal Son — freely, why would He withhold anything of lesser value? The cross is the foundation of unshakeable trust in God's goodness in every circumstance.

Returning to the Cross — Disciplines for Daily and Weekly Life

  • Read one of the four crucifixion accounts (Matt. 26–27, Mark 14–15, Luke 22–23, John 18–19) slowly once a week, as if present at the scene
  • Meditate on one verse of Isaiah 53 per day for a month — let each phrase land fully before moving on
  • When guilt or spiritual distance is felt, go to the cross first — not to try harder, but to receive. Grace comes before effort.
  • Observe the Lord's Supper as a deliberate meditation on Calvary, not a ritual to complete — "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19)
  • When beholding another person's need or suffering, ask: "What did God give up to redeem this person?" — it deepens both compassion and love for God

Section IX

Gratitude & Praise

"Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name. Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits." — Psalm 103:1–2

Gratitude is simultaneously a fruit of love for God and one of the most powerful channels through which love grows. The soul that cultivates thankfulness becomes increasingly aware of God's presence and goodness in every dimension of life — and awareness of God's goodness is the seedbed of deeper love. Conversely, the soul that allows itself to focus on lack, complaint, and disappointment gradually becomes blind to the God who is constantly surrounding it with mercy.

EGW was remarkably insistent on this — she treated gratitude not as a nice spiritual accent but as a moral and spiritual obligation, a spiritual discipline with measurable effects on the health of the soul. The Psalms model it extravagantly: 150 chapters in which praise, lament, and thanksgiving weave together in an unbreakable cord of relational honesty with God.

הָלַל
Hebrew
Halal
ha-LAHL — Strong's H1984
The root of "Hallelujah" — to praise, to boast, to celebrate with great enthusiasm. It is exuberant, unrestrained praise. The word carries the idea of shining, of reflecting light. To praise God is to reflect His glory back to its source. Used 165 times in the Old Testament, it reaches its peak in the final five Psalms (146–150), each of which opens and closes with "Hallelujah." God does not call for reluctant, quiet acknowledgment — He calls for exuberant, joyful, shining praise.
Psalm 100:4 — Thanksgiving as the Gate
"Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise: be thankful unto him, and bless his name."
The temple had literal gates and courts — progressively inward spaces of increasing holiness. The Psalmist maps the spiritual journey into God's presence onto this architecture: you enter through thanksgiving. It is not that you arrive in God's presence and then feel grateful — you come through gratitude to arrive in His courts. Thanksgiving is the doorway.
Philippians 4:6–7 — The Peace That Follows Thankful Prayer
"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus."
Note that thanksgiving is embedded in the petition itself — not added as an afterthought. Paul instructs that every request be accompanied by thanksgiving. This disciplines the heart to see God's goodness even while presenting its needs. The result is not just answered prayer but peace — the "peace that passes understanding."
1 Thessalonians 5:18 — In Everything
"In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you."
"In everything" — not for everything (we are not commanded to be grateful for evil itself) but in every circumstance, maintaining a posture of trust that God is sovereign and good even when the situation is not. This is advanced gratitude — the thanksgiving that operates in darkness as well as light.
Acts 16:25 — Praise in the Prison
"And at midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God: and the prisoners heard them."
Midnight. After flogging. In stocks. Inner prison. And they sang. This is not cheerful denial of suffering — it is the active, deliberate choice to worship God in the worst circumstances. The result: an earthquake, open doors, a conversion. God answered the praise with power.
Ellen G. White — The Ministry of Healing
"Nothing tends more to promote health of body and of soul than does a spirit of gratitude and praise. It is a positive duty to resist melancholy, discontented thoughts and feelings — as much a duty as it is to pray. If we would have the blessing of God, we must cultivate cheerfulness; we must praise Him more."
— The Ministry of Healing, p. 251
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"Sing more, and talk less of your difficulties and discouragements. Praise God more. If you would have the joy of the Lord, cultivate gratitude... Those who are always sighing and complaining are robbing themselves of spiritual power. You cannot afford to grieve. Praise is a key that unlocks heaven's storehouses."
— Steps to Christ, p. 103 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 5
"Gratitude is the fairest blossom which springs from the soul. It is not alone the poverty of the gifts we receive that fails to produce gratitude, but the poverty of our perception of those gifts... We are surrounded every moment with mercies too numerous to count, and too vast to measure."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 5, p. 317 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 1
"In my travels I have met with those who seemed to think it a mark of humility to mourn and complain... But this is not humility — it is unbelief. True humility is grateful. — Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 1, p. 702 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Gospel Workers
"Music is one of the most effective means of impressing the heart with spiritual truth... Singing is as much an act of worship as prayer. Fill the home with music. Let the children learn hymns. Let praise be the atmosphere of the household as well as the church."

"Praise, with gratitude to God, the Maker of heaven and earth, will keep the heart young and joyous. To count your blessings, to tell of His mercies, to rehearse the goodness of God — this is not sentiment. It is the exercise of the most powerful spiritual faculty available to the human soul."
— Education, p. 159 (paraphrased)
Gratitude Disciplines That Build Love for God
  • Evening gratitude review: Before sleep, name five specific things God gave or did that day — not generic ("life and breath") but particular ("He gave me patience when I was provoked," "He sent that person to encourage me at exactly the right moment"). Specificity deepens the sense of God's personal involvement in your life.
  • Keep a praise journal: Write answered prayers, mercies noticed, moments of unexpected beauty or help. Review it when darkness comes — the accumulated record of God's faithfulness becomes an anchor for faith. "I have not forgotten your mercies of former years." (Lam. 3:22 paraphrased)
  • Begin every prayer session with extended praise: Spend the first five minutes of prayer solely on who God is and what He has done — before any petition. The Lord's Prayer models this structure: "Hallowed be thy name" before "give us this day." This disciplines the heart to approach God as Giver before Responder.
  • Praise specifically in trial: In hardship, identify one thing God is doing or one attribute of His character that is trustworthy in this situation. Thank Him for that specifically, even if you cannot yet see the outcome. This is the "sacrifice of praise" (Heb. 13:15) — offered when it costs something.
  • Sing praise deliberately and regularly: Singing engages emotion and will simultaneously in a way spoken prayer alone does not. "Singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord." (Eph. 5:19). Keep hymns and worship music as the soundtrack of your daily life as much as possible.
  • Tell others what God has done: "Come and hear, all ye that fear God, and I will declare what he hath done for my soul." (Ps. 66:16). Bearing witness to God's goodness deepens your own awareness of it. Testimony reinforces memory, and memory of God's goodness builds love.

Section X

Nature as a School of Love

"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge." — Psalm 19:1–2

EGW held a theology of nature that was more robust, more intentional, and more spiritually practical than almost any contemporary voice. She did not view the natural world as a pleasant backdrop for human activity — she viewed it as God's second book, an ongoing revelation of His character that complements and illuminates the written Word. In an age of screens, artificiality, and indoor disconnection, this aspect of her counsel is perhaps more urgently needed than at any time since she wrote it.

God's original school for humanity was a garden (Gen. 2:8). His original Sabbath context was the completion of creation (Gen. 2:1–3). His original instruction for the human race was to tend, name, and engage with the living world around them. Disconnection from nature is not spiritually neutral — it cuts us off from one of God's primary channels of revelation and love.

Psalm 19:1–4 — Creation as Continuous Speech
"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. Their line is gone out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world."
Creation speaks constantly — "day unto day uttereth speech." It is not a one-time message delivered at creation and then silent. It is ongoing declaration, moment by moment, in every language and every landscape. The question is whether we are listening.
Romans 1:20 — Invisible Things Made Visible
"For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse."
Paul's claim is remarkable: God's invisible attributes — His eternal power and divine nature — are clearly seen through what He has made. The created order is not a hint of God; it is a clear revelation. Theology can be done outdoors, not only in libraries.
Job 12:7–9 — Ask the Animals
"But ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee; and the fowls of the air, and they shall tell thee: Or speak to the earth, and it shall teach thee: and the fishes of the sea shall declare unto thee. Who knoweth not in all these that the hand of the LORD hath wrought this?"
Matthew 6:26–29 — Jesus' Theology From Nature
"Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns; yet your heavenly Father feedeth them... Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin: And yet I say unto you, That even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these."
Jesus habitually drew theological lessons from the natural world — birds, seeds, vines, sheep, wolves, weather, harvest. His parables are saturated with creation imagery. He was teaching His disciples to read God's character in what surrounded them every day.
Ellen G. White — Education
"Through nature and revelation, through His providence, and by the influence of the Holy Spirit, God speaks to us... Every budding flower, every dewdrop and gentle rain, the solemn grandeur of the hills, the mighty ocean, and countless orbs of night — all these speak to us of God's love and power."
— Education, p. 99
Ellen G. White — The Desire of Ages
"The Sabbath calls our thoughts to nature, and brings us into communion with the Creator. In the song of the bird, the sighing of the trees, and the music of the sea, we still may hear His voice who talked with Adam in Eden in the cool of the day. And nature still speaks to us of His tenderness and His love."
— The Desire of Ages, p. 281
Ellen G. White — The Ministry of Healing
"There is healing in the natural world for the wounds of the soul. In contact with nature there is a restoration of that peace which so many have lost... God is approached more readily through nature than through the artificialities of human construction. He has placed His signature on every leaf, every stone, every creature."
— The Ministry of Healing, pp. 59–60 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 6
"Those who live in close contact with nature are refreshed and invigorated. Outdoor life is one of the most effective means of combating the depression and discouragement that so often attend spiritual decline. Open the windows of the soul to nature's classroom."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 6, p. 178 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"Let the student of nature turn his eyes to the God of nature, and a tide of love will flow from the heart of the Infinite to the finite... The flowers, the grass, the trees, the brooks, the very stones, are a language in which God speaks to us of His mercy, His tenderness, His care."
— Steps to Christ, p. 86
Ellen G. White — Child Guidance
"Parents should take their children into the garden and the field, and point out to them the beautiful things God has made... Let children be taught that God made all of these things in love — that not one sparrow falls without His notice. This is the theology of childhood that produces the devotion of adulthood."
— Child Guidance, p. 45 (paraphrased)
How to Read Nature Theologically

The next time you are outdoors, choose one object — a tree, a flower, an ant colony, a bird, a sunset, a river — and ask deliberately: What does this reveal about its Maker? A tree's invisible root system sustaining what is visible above — the hidden life of faith. Seeds that must die to produce life — the theology of the cross encoded in botany. The sun that rises every morning without fail — the faithfulness of God who does not sleep. The sunset's gratuitous, unnecessary beauty, given freely to every creature every evening — the extravagance of divine love. These are not merely pleasant observations. They are love letters from a God who cannot stop expressing Himself through everything He makes.

Section XI

Service & Loving Others

"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me." — Matthew 25:40

One of the most counterintuitive but consistently verified truths in Christian spirituality is this: you do not grow in love for God by turning inward on yourself, even religiously. Self-examination taken to excess becomes a self-reinforcing spiral of introspection. But the soul that turns outward in service — that pours itself out for others — consistently finds God meeting it there in the service, and returns from service with a deeper love than it left with.

EGW returned to this truth across her writings with extraordinary consistency. It is the cure for doubt, depression, spiritual stagnation, backsliding, and cold love. Service is not the reward for spiritual maturity — it is the means of producing it.

Matthew 25:35–40 — Christ Encountered in the Needy
"For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in: naked, and ye clothed me: I was sick, and ye visited me: I was in prison, and ye came unto me... Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."
Jesus does not say He is represented by the suffering — He says He is the suffering. When you serve a hungry person, you are literally ministering to Christ. This transforms service from duty into encounter. You do not merely help the poor — you find Christ in the serving.
1 John 4:20 — The Test That Cannot Be Faked
"If a man say, I love God, and hateth his brother, he is a liar: for he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?"
James 2:14–17 — Faith Made Visible in Service
"What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? can faith save him? If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food, and one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?"
Isaiah 58:6–8 — The Fast God Chooses
"Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free... Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house? when thou seest the naked, that thou cover him... Then shall thy light break forth as the morning, and thine health shall spring forth speedily."
God explicitly links outward service to inward spiritual light. "Then shall thy light break forth" — after and because of the service. The light of spiritual experience is the consequence of outward, practical love for the suffering.
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"If you will go to work as Christ designs that His disciples shall, and win souls for Him, you will feel the need of a deeper experience and a greater knowledge in divine things, and will hunger and thirst after righteousness. You will plead with God, and your faith will be strengthened, and your soul will drink deeper drafts at the well of salvation."
— Steps to Christ, p. 80
Ellen G. White — The Ministry of Healing
"The soul that is sluggish and cold in spiritual things will find quickening power in service. It is not by contemplation alone that the soul grows strong, but by active effort in behalf of others. As the hand that is constantly in use grows stronger, so the spiritual faculties that are constantly employed in ministry grow stronger."
— The Ministry of Healing, p. 148 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 2
"Doing good is the best remedy for the doubting and the despairing. When the dark moods come, when faith wavers and love grows cold — do not sit and wait for the feeling to change. Rise and serve. Visit the sick. Relieve the poor. Encourage the discouraged. The warmth will return in the doing."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 2, p. 135 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Christ's Object Lessons
"The talents are to be used for the benefit of the world. This is the condition of their growth. A talent withheld is a talent that atrophies. Love unexpressed is love that diminishes. Serve, and love multiplies. Withhold, and love contracts. The law of the kingdom is that giving increases."
— Christ's Object Lessons, p. 356 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 5
"Prayer and effort, effort and prayer, will be the business of your life. You must pray as though the efficiency were all in God, and labour as though it were all in you. Both are true simultaneously. And the combination produces a depth of love and a breadth of usefulness that neither alone can generate."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 5, p. 200 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 9
"God has given to every man his work. The very humblest worker in the vineyard of the Lord is as much on a mission from God as the apostle Paul. The errand boy who runs his message, the domestic who sweeps the floor, the mother who rocks her child — all, if acting in the fear of God and love of man, are doing the will of God."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 9, p. 130 (paraphrased)

Service as the Cure for Spiritual Stagnation

Much spiritual coldness is the product of inward focus — endless self-examination, worry about spiritual state, preoccupation with one's own needs. EGW's prescription is consistent and almost shockingly simple: get up and serve someone. Not after you feel spiritual. Now, regardless of feeling.

Practical outlets this week: visit one person who is sick or lonely. Prepare a meal for a family under stress. Write a letter or message of genuine encouragement to someone struggling. Give something away that costs you something. Share your faith with one person — not with a pamphlet but with your own story. Volunteer at a food kitchen, a prison ministry, a community garden.

You will return from these acts of service with more love for God than you left with. This is not theory — it is the consistent testimony of everyone who has tried it.

Section XII

Trials & the Furnace of Growth

"That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ." — 1 Peter 1:7

No serious treatment of spiritual growth can sidestep suffering. Trials are not the enemy of love for God — they are, handled rightly, among its most powerful and irreplaceable catalysts. The love produced in shallow prosperity is shallow love. The love forged in the furnace of genuine trial — the kind that holds through confusion, through unanswered prayer, through loss — is a love that eternity itself cannot shake.

EGW's theology of suffering is neither stoic denial nor passive resignation. It is active, interpretive trust — the decision to keep the eyes on God's character in the midst of what cannot yet be explained. And it is grounded in the consistent biblical narrative that God's greatest servants were almost universally shaped by severe trial.

Romans 5:3–5 — The Theology of Suffering
"And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; and patience, experience; and experience, hope: and hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us."
The chain is precise: tribulation → patience (endurance) → proven character → hope → and the destination is "the love of God shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Spirit." Suffering, surrendered to God and endured in faith, leads directly to a greater experience of God's love — not as consolation prize, but as direct consequence.
Job 23:10 — Gold from the Fire
"But he knoweth the way that I take: when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold."
Job speaks this in the depths of his suffering — after he has lost everything, while he cannot find God, while his friends condemn him. Yet his faith holds: when He has tried me (not if, but when — certainty of outcome), I shall come forth as gold. This is not pretence — it is the most courageous act of faith in the Old Testament.
Hebrews 12:6–11 — Discipline as Evidence of Sonship
"For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth... Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby."
The word "afterward" is everything. The fruit is not present-tense but future-tense — it is yielded through and after the discipline. This requires patience: the willingness to trust God's purpose before the fruit is visible.
2 Corinthians 4:17 — Weight of Glory
"For our light and momentary troubles are achieving for us an eternal glory that far outweighs them all."
Paul uses the language of weight — trials have weight, but glory has far greater weight. And the striking word is "achieving" (katergazetai) — the affliction is actively producing, working out, accomplishing the glory. Suffering is not merely endured — it is productive.
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 5
"The very trials that test our faith most severely are designed to strengthen it. God leads His people by a way they know not, but He knows the way. And when they have been sufficiently tested, He will bring forth their character as gold tried in the fire. Every soul who has gone through deep trial and emerged holding God's hand has a story that shallow comfort could never produce."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 5, p. 212
Ellen G. White — The Ministry of Healing
"In the school of trial, God would teach us lessons that we could not learn in any other way. In the furnace of affliction, the dross of self and pride and worldliness is burned away, and the gold of character is purified and made more precious. The Father's hand holds the chisel. The furnace is hot. But the gold does not suffer — only the dross does."
— The Ministry of Healing, p. 489 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — The Great Controversy
"The darkness of suffering is often God's light seen from behind. As Moses in the cleft of the rock saw God's back as He passed — not His full face — so in the midst of trial we see only the trailing edge of His glory. The full face is for after the trial. But the back of God is still God."
— The Great Controversy, p. 621 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Patriarchs and Prophets
"The history of Joseph is a history of a soul who grew in love for God through every trial — in the pit, in Potiphar's house, in the prison. At no point did he have answers. At every point he had God. And what came out of that furnace was not bitterness but wisdom, not hatred but forgiveness, not cynicism but the deepest trust in divine sovereignty that the Old Testament records."
— Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 239 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 4
"God does not willingly afflict or grieve the children of men. If we could see, as God sees, how great eternal results are being wrought out through the trials of life, we would not desire those trials removed, but would say with full heart: 'It is enough that what grieves me pleases God. His will be done.'"
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 4, p. 527 (paraphrased)
The Question in the Furnace

When trial comes, the deepest question is not "Why is God doing this?" but "Who is God in this?" The three Hebrew young men in Daniel 3 did not know whether they would survive the furnace — but they knew the character of the God they served. "Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us... but if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods." (Dan. 3:17–18). And in the furnace, they encountered a Fourth Person. You do not encounter that Presence in comfort. You encounter Him in fire.

Section XIII

Common Obstacles to Love for God

"Nevertheless I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first love." — Revelation 2:4

The church at Ephesus had correct doctrine, disciplined practice, patient endurance under persecution, and a tested ability to identify false teaching. By almost every external measure it was a model church. And yet Jesus had one devastating charge: thou hast left thy first love. It is possible — distressingly possible — to maintain all the forms of religion while the heart has quietly drifted from God. EGW identified the specific thieves of love with prophetic precision across her writings.

The Cares of This Life

"Choked with cares and riches and pleasures of this life, and bring no fruit to perfection." (Luke 8:14). Busyness is the most socially acceptable and most spiritually lethal killer of love for God. The remedy is not less work but deliberately protected time with God — time that cannot be negotiated away by urgent things.

Unconfessed and Cherished Sin

"If I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me." (Ps. 66:18). Sin does not merely break God's law — it distorts the soul's perception of God. Cherished sin creates a spiritual blindfold. The remedy is not self-condemnation but honest, specific confession and the acceptance of forgiveness that God has promised.

Spiritual Pride and Self-Sufficiency

EGW: "The Laodicean message is given to a church that thinks it is rich and increased with goods and in need of nothing — but is in fact wretched, poor, blind, and naked." (Rev. 3:17). The soul confident in its own spiritual attainments stops hungering. Pride of knowledge or spiritual standing is a stone door against love.

Dead Formalism

Going through the motions — prayer without heart, church attendance without engagement, Bible reading without expectation. EGW called this "the most dangerous spiritual state": having the form of godliness while denying the power (2 Tim. 3:5). It produces comfort without connection and dulls the capacity for genuine spiritual experience.

Media Saturation and Sensory Overload

EGW warned repeatedly that the mind fed on exciting, violent, or sensual content loses its capacity for spiritual impression. "The mind is the medium through which God reaches the soul. Dull or corrupt it, and the channel is blocked." (Testimonies, Vol. 5, p. 543, paraphrased). The spiritual atrophy of excessive screen time is one of the most urgent modern applications of her counsel.

Holding Unforgiveness and Grudges

"If ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." (Matt. 6:15). Unforgiveness is not merely a moral failing — it closes the heart at its deepest point. A heart that will not open fully to a human being it can see cannot fully open to God whom it cannot see. Forgiveness is a prerequisite of deep intimacy with God.

Comparing Yourself to Others

The soul that measures its spiritual state against other people's visible piety will become either proud or discouraged — neither of which leads toward God. EGW: "Keep the eye fixed on Jesus, not on your brother. Christ is the standard. Everything else is irrelevant as a measure." The comparison game always distracts from the only Comparison that matters.

Neglecting Known Light

"To him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin." (James 4:17). Disobedience to known light dims the spiritual perception that would receive more light. Love grows in the direction of obedience and atrophies in the direction of wilful neglect. Every time you act on what you know, love deepens. Every time you suppress it, the capacity diminishes.

Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 5
"The first steps of apostasy are taken when men substitute their own plans for God's... Spiritual decline is almost always gradual — first one small compromise, then another, then a rationalisation, then a habit. By the time the soul realises how far it has drifted, the current is strong. The remedy must be equally decisive: stop, turn, return."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 5, p. 217 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 4
"We lose much by not maintaining a constant, living connection with God. We should cultivate the knowledge of Him. We should not be content to know that the theory of truth is correct while the heart has grown cold toward the God of that truth."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 4, p. 394

Section XIV

Reviving Cold Love

"Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works." — Revelation 2:5

Spiritual coldness is not unusual — it is nearly universal at some season of the Christian life. The most honest saints in history have written about it: David in the Psalms, Jeremiah in his laments, John of the Cross in the Dark Night of the Soul, and EGW herself acknowledged that the spiritual life has seasons of winter. The question is not whether cold love will happen — it is what to do when it does.

Jesus' prescription to Ephesus is remarkably specific, practically actionable, and mercifully brief. Three words: remember, repent, return. There is no lengthy programme, no extended qualification process, no season of suffering required before restoration. Just three steps, available today.

Revelation 2:4–5 — The Three-Part Prescription
"Nevertheless I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first love. Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his place, except thou repent."
Hosea 6:1–3 — The Call to Return
"Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up. After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight. Then shall we know, if we follow on to know the LORD."
"Follow on to know" — the knowledge of God deepens by following Him, not merely by studying about Him. Return is both intellectual (remember) and volitional (repent) and practical (do the first works — follow on).
Lamentations 3:21–23 — Returning to Hope in Darkness
"This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope. It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."
Jeremiah writes this in the ashes of Jerusalem — surrounded by devastation, grief, and apparent divine abandonment. And yet he makes a deliberate decision: "This I recall to my mind" — he chooses what to think about. He reaches for mercy rather than despair. This is the first move of recovering love: deliberate remembrance of God's faithfulness.
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"If you feel that you have no love for Christ, and yet desire this love, it is evidence that He is drawing you... The desire to be in harmony with God is the beginning of recovery. Do not wait until you feel right. Act right, and feeling will follow. Go to God in the poverty of your soul, without anything to recommend you, and cast yourself on His grace."
— Steps to Christ, pp. 47, 64 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 5
"When the soul that has wandered returns to God, it will find Him waiting. The prodigal while yet a great way off — still in rags, still smelling of the swine — was seen by the father who ran to meet him. God does not wait at the finish line of recovery — He runs toward you while you are still on the road back."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 5, p. 635 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Review and Herald, Nov. 1, 1892
"The backslider need not regard himself as beyond the reach of help. He has not sinned away his day of grace as long as he feels drawn toward God. The very drawing is the Holy Spirit at work. Come back. Come now. Every step toward God is met with many steps by God toward you."
— Review and Herald, November 1, 1892 (paraphrased)
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 1
"Return in memory to your first love. What were you doing when love was warm? Reading the Word daily? Praying with earnestness? Attending church with expectation? Serving others? Witnessing? Return to those practices — not to earn restoration, but as the channels through which restoration flows. The means of grace are the channels of grace."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 1, p. 155 (paraphrased)
1

Remember

Go back in memory to when your love was alive. What were you doing? What were you reading? Who were you spending time with? What were your daily rhythms of prayer and Scripture? What was the quality of your attention to God? Compare that season with the present. The contrast itself is the beginning of returning — you cannot grieve what you do not remember.

2

Repent

Not flogging yourself with guilt — that is not repentance, it is self-punishment. Repentance is honest reorientation: "Lord, I have drifted. I have let other things crowd You out. I have preferred my comfort to Your presence. I come back." The prodigal said "I will arise and go to my father" — and then he arose and went. Repentance is completed in the movement, not the feeling.

3

Do the First Works

Return to the specific practices that marked your early love — even without the feeling. Open the Bible. Pray aloud. Attend church. Serve someone this week. Read The Desire of Ages. Take a prayer walk. Contact that friend you have been avoiding. Fast one meal and spend that time with God. Act in the direction of love, and love will follow. EGW: "The feelings are the caboose, not the engine. The will drives the train."

— Hosea 14:4

— Malachi 3:7

Section XV

A Daily Rhythm of Growing Love

"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." — Lamentations 3:22–23

Love for God does not grow in theory — it grows in daily, specific, embodied practice. EGW was deeply and consistently practical: the spiritual life has rhythms and disciplines, and without them it withers regardless of intention or emotional sincerity. She was not legalistic about these rhythms — she was insistent about them because she understood the nature of the human soul in its present condition: it requires structure, it requires habits, it requires the repeated placing of itself before God to remain open to God.

The following rhythm is synthesised from across her writings, Scripture, and the practical wisdom of the whole tradition of Christian spirituality. It is a framework, not a formula — adapt it to your season, your responsibilities, your temperament. But do not abandon it entirely. The shape matters even when the form varies.

Lamentations 3:22–23 — New Every Morning
"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."
God's compassions (Hebrew: rachamim — womb-like tender mercies) are renewed every morning. Each day God presses fresh grace toward us. Each morning is a new beginning — not a continuation of yesterday's failure but a fresh outpouring of new mercy. The rhythm of daily renewal is built into the structure of divine compassion itself.
Ellen G. White — Steps to Christ
"Consecrate yourself to God in the morning; make this your very first work. Let your prayer be, 'Take me, O Lord, as wholly Thine. I lay all my plans at Thy feet. Use me today in Thy service. Abide with me, and let all my work be wrought in Thee.' This is a daily matter. Each morning consecrate yourself to God for that day. Surrender all your plans to Him, to be carried out or given up as His providence shall indicate."
— Steps to Christ, p. 70
Ellen G. White — Testimonies, Vol. 7
"Every morning we should present ourselves, our souls and bodies, to God and ask for the grace of the Holy Spirit to work in us mightily... Not only in the morning, but at noon, at night, and at all times, the soul needs to be kept humble and trusting before God, dependent upon divine grace and willing to be led and controlled by the Spirit."
— Testimonies for the Church, Vol. 7, p. 44
Ellen G. White — Messages to Young People
"The hours of morning and evening devotion are precious beyond calculation. Begin the day with God. Close the day with God. Let nothing crowd these appointments out. Every other part of the day will go better for having begun and ended in His presence."
— Messages to Young People, p. 337 (paraphrased)

A Full Daily Rhythm

🌅

First Moments — Consecration

Before the phone, before news, before conversation — give the first conscious moments to God. A brief prayer of consecration: "Lord, this day is Yours. I am Yours. Lead me." This anchors the will before the world sets its agenda.

📖

Morning — Scripture

20–30 minutes minimum in the Word. Read slowly. Read one passage and stay with it rather than racing through chapters. Ask: what does this show me about God? Journal one observation. Let the Word shape the day's mindset.

🙏

Morning — Prayer

Begin with praise (who God is). Move to gratitude (what He has done). Confess honestly. Intercede for five specific people by name. Present your own needs. Listen. Keep a prayer journal — record what you prayed and when God answers.

🌿

Throughout the Day — Breath Prayers

"Pray without ceasing" (1 Thess. 5:17) lived practically: short, arrow prayers all day. "Lord, help me here." "Thank you for that." "Give me wisdom in this meeting." "I trust you with this worry." The day becomes a sustained conversation rather than a secular interruption between two prayer sessions.

🤝

Afternoon — One Act of Service

Every day, one intentional act of love toward another person. It need not be large. Encourage one person specifically. Give something. Listen without interrupting. These small daily acts of service keep love flowing outward and prevent spiritual stagnation.

🌄

Evening — Review and Gratitude

Before sleep, review the day with God. Where did you see Him at work? What are you specifically grateful for? What needs to be confessed before you sleep? Rest in His forgiveness. Do not carry tomorrow's burdens into sleep — "casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you." (1 Pet. 5:7)

🎵

Throughout — Sacred Music

EGW: "Cultivate the love of sacred music. It is one of the most effective means of impressing the heart with spiritual truth." (Gospel Workers, p. 358). Let hymns and worship music fill the silence of the day — while cooking, commuting, working. The singing heart is a heart turned toward God.

📚

Weekly — EGW Devotional Reading

Alongside Scripture, read one chapter of The Desire of Ages, Steps to Christ, or Thoughts from the Mount of Blessing per week as devotional reading — not study, but reception. These books are designed to do what they do in the heart of a reader who comes to them open and unhurried.
