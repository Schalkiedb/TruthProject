The Hebrew & Greek Words
What "sanctify" actually means in the original languages

The Core Meaning: Separation for Sacred Purpose

The English word "sanctify" is often reduced to a vague idea of moral improvement. But the original Hebrew root is far more concrete: it means to cut something out from the common and dedicate it wholly to God. Sanctification begins not with the sinner's effort but with God's act of setting apart — and the very first thing God ever sanctified in Scripture was not a person, not a priest, not an altar. It was a day.

Hebrew — Old Testament

קָדַשׁ
Qādash — Strong's H6942
Pronunciation: kaw-dash
Primary meanings: to be holy, to consecrate, to set apart, to dedicate, to purify, to hallow

Total KJV occurrences (verb form): 172 — rendered as: sanctify (108×), hallow (25×), dedicate (10×), holy (7×), prepare (7×), consecrate (5×), and various others

Related noun forms:
• Qodesh (קֹדֶשׁ) — "holiness / the holy thing" — 468 occurrences
• Qādôsh (קָדוֹשׁ) — "holy" (adjective) — 116 occurrences

The root idea: separation from the common (chōl) for exclusive use by, or belonging to, God. A thing sanctified does not become morally better on its own — it is relocated into God's realm. The moral transformation follows from the relational position.

Greek — New Testament

ἁγιάζω
Hagiazō — Strong's G37
Pronunciation: hag-ee-ad-zo
Primary meanings: to make holy, to purify, to consecrate, to separate for God's use

NT occurrences: ~29 in the verb form; related noun hagiasmos (sanctification) appears 10×

Key distinction from Hebrew: The Greek hagiazō is closer in meaning to the Hebrew qodesh (the holy thing itself) than to qādash (the act of setting apart). In the NT, "sanctify" almost always means the active work of making holy — it is more explicitly transformational, not merely positional.

Agent: In the NT, sanctification is uniformly God's work — through Christ (Heb 2:11), through the Spirit (2 Thess 2:13), and through truth (John 17:17). The human role is faith, obedience, and cooperation — not the source of the holiness.
✦ The Name of God ✦

YHWH Mekoddishkem — "The LORD Who Sanctifies You"

In Exodus 31:13 and Ezekiel 20:12, God reveals one of the most concentrated names for Himself in all of Scripture: Jehovah Mekoddishkem — "The LORD Who Sanctifies You." The name appears only twice. Both times it is attached directly to the Sabbath as the sign by which His people are to know Him in this capacity. The name is not abstract — it is tied to an institution, a day, and a covenant relationship. God defines Himself as the Sanctifier, and He assigns the Sabbath as the sign that communicates this identity to His people.

📜
The Sabbath — First to Be Sanctified
Qādash appears first in Genesis 2:3 — before priests, altars, or temples

The Structural Weight of "First Use"

In Hebrew exegesis, the first use of a word establishes its foundational meaning for all subsequent uses. The word qādash — sanctify — appears for the first time in the entire Bible in Genesis 2:3. Not in Leviticus. Not at Sinai. Not in a priestly regulation. God's inaugural act of sanctification in all of human history is the setting apart of the seventh day. This is not coincidental. Before sin existed. Before the sacrificial system. Before Israel. Before Moses. God defined what sanctification looked like by sanctifying time.

Furthermore, the Sabbath is the only thing qādash'd in the book of Genesis. After Genesis 2:3, the word disappears from Genesis entirely — it does not reappear until Exodus 13, when the firstborn are sanctified. The Sabbath stands alone in the first book of Scripture as the sole object of divine sanctification.

Genesis 2:3 — The First Sanctification
After six days of creation, before any human sin or priestly system
"Then God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it [qādash], because in it He rested from all His work which God had created and made."
This is the first occurrence of qādash in the entire Bible. God does not sanctify Adam, Eve, the garden, or the animals — He sanctifies time itself. Holiness enters creation through the Sabbath. EGW: "God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it — set it apart to a holy use. He gave it to Adam as a day of rest. It was a memorial of the work of creation, and thus a sign of God's power and His love." (Desire of Ages, p. 281)
Exodus 16:23 — Before Sinai
The manna test — God identifies the Sabbath before the Law is formally given
"Tomorrow is to be a day of sabbath rest, a holy sabbath to the Lord… bake what you want to bake and boil what you want to boil. Save whatever is left and keep it until morning."
The double portion of manna on Friday and God's instruction to keep the Sabbath holy — before the giving of the Ten Commandments — shows the Sabbath was not introduced at Sinai. It was restored at Sinai. Israel had lost the Sabbath in Egypt. God reintroduced it through the manna test as the very first commandment He tested them on (Exod 16:4).
Exodus 20:8–11 — Fourth Commandment
The Ten Commandments — Sabbath command tied to creation
"Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy [qādash]… For in six days the Lord made the heavens and the earth… and rested the seventh day. Therefore the Lord blessed the Sabbath day and hallowed [qādash] it."
The fourth commandment uses qādash twice. The human obligation ("keep it holy") is grounded in the divine action ("He hallowed it"). Man sanctifies the Sabbath by recognising and aligning with what God has already declared holy — not by making it holy through ritual. Deuteronomy 5:15 adds the second foundation: remembrance of redemption from Egypt — creation and salvation are both enshrined in the Sabbath.
Nehemiah 13:22 — Men Sanctify the Sabbath
Nehemiah confronted Sabbath profanation in restored Jerusalem
"And I commanded the Levites that they should cleanse themselves and that they should go and guard the gates, to sanctify [qādash] the Sabbath day."
Here men sanctify the Sabbath — which means they guard what God has already declared holy. Human sanctification of the Sabbath is not creating its holiness but honoring and protecting it. This is precisely the human side of the Sabbath covenant: God sanctifies us; we sanctify the day by keeping it as He commanded.
Ellen G. White — Desire of Ages, p. 281–283
"The Sabbath calls our thoughts to nature, and brings us into communion with the Creator. In the song of the bird, the sighing of the trees, and the music of the sea, we still may hear His voice who talked with Adam in Eden in the cool of the day. And as we behold His power in nature, we find comfort, for the word that created all things is that which speaks life to the soul… He who rested upon the Sabbath after the work of creation was not weary… When we celebrate the Sabbath, we commemorate that work. The Sabbath is not a day of mourning — it is a feast day. It was made for man that he might rejoice in the Creator's works."
Desire of Ages, pp. 281–283 (composite)

The Sabbath as the Sign of Sanctification
Four times Scripture explicitly links the Sabbath with God's sanctifying work

A Sign That Communicates God's Identity as Sanctifier

The Hebrew word for "sign" here is ôth (אוֹת) — the same word used for the rainbow covenant (Gen 9:13), for circumcision (Gen 17:11), and for the Passover blood on the doorposts (Exod 12:13). These are not decorative symbols — they are covenant seals: marks that identify the relationship between two parties. God attaches the word ôth to the Sabbath specifically in the context of sanctification, meaning: the Sabbath is the visible mark that God is the one doing the work of making His people holy. It is not the mark of Israel's effort — it is the mark of God's power operating in them.

Exodus 31:13 — Sign #1
God speaks to Moses at Sinai regarding the Sabbath's deeper meaning
"Surely My Sabbaths you shall keep, for it is a sign between Me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I am the Lord who sanctifies you [YHWH Mekoddishkem]."
The first explicit linking of Sabbath and sanctification. The sign points not primarily to Israel's obedience but to God's identity as the Sanctifier. Sabbath-keeping is the evidence — the "mark" — that the keeper belongs to the One who has the power and intention to make them holy. Note: throughout your generations — no time limit is placed on this sign.
Exodus 31:17 — Sign #2
God restates the Sabbath sign — this time as a covenant between Himself and Israel forever
"It is a sign between Me and the children of Israel forever; for in six days the Lord made the heavens and the earth, and on the seventh day He rested and was refreshed."
The word forever (עוֹלָם, olam) is the same word used for the Abrahamic and Davidic covenants. The Sabbath sign is not provisional or Levitical — it is rooted in creation itself. The refreshment of God on the Sabbath ("was refreshed" — nāphash, lit. "breathed/had soul restored") is a window into the relational depth the day was designed to communicate.
Ezekiel 20:12 — Sign #3
Ezekiel recounts Israel's history to the elders in Babylon — the Sabbath was the test they kept failing
"Moreover I also gave them My Sabbaths, to be a sign between them and Me, that they might know that I am the Lord who sanctifies them. Yet the house of Israel rebelled against Me in the wilderness… and they greatly defiled My Sabbaths."
God's diagnosis of Israel's repeated apostasy is Sabbath desecration. Not idolatry as a separate issue — but Sabbath-breaking as the indicator that they had rejected the God who sanctifies. The profaned Sabbath was the sign of a broken sanctification relationship. EGW noted this pattern extensively — the loss of the Sabbath always precedes spiritual apostasy.
Ezekiel 20:20 — Sign #4
God calls the exiles to return — restoration begins with Sabbath restoration
"Hallow My Sabbaths, and they will be a sign between Me and you, that you may know that I am the Lord your God."
The restoration of the covenant relationship begins with the restoration of Sabbath-keeping. This is not coincidental — the Sabbath is the sign of the sanctification relationship. When the sign is repaired, the relationship is repaired. God's instruction for recovery from national apostasy was not more sacrifice — it was return to the Sabbath.

What the Sign Says in Both Directions

A covenant sign functions in two directions. When Israel kept the Sabbath, it said to God: "We acknowledge You as our Sanctifier — we are Yours." And God's response was to say: "They are mine — I am working in them." When Israel broke the Sabbath, it severed both sides of the communication. The sign was defaced. This explains the severity of Sabbath violations in Scripture — it was not about the day as such, but about the covenant relationship the day embodied.

Ellen G. White — Counsels for the Church, p. 261
"The Sabbath given to the world as the sign of God as the Creator is also the sign of Him as the Sanctifier. The power that created all things is the power that re-creates the soul in His own likeness. To those who keep holy the Sabbath day it is the sign of sanctification. True sanctification is harmony with God, oneness with Him in character. It is received through obedience to those principles that are the transcript of His character. And the Sabbath is the sign of obedience. He who from the heart obeys the fourth commandment will obey the whole law. He is sanctified through obedience."
Counsels for the Church, p. 261 (CCh 261.4)

How to Read This Survey

The 172 OT occurrences of qādash cover four main categories: God sanctifying things (Sabbath, tabernacle, altar, offerings), God sanctifying people (priests, firstborn, Israel as a nation), people sanctifying themselves (preparation for encounter with God), and people sanctifying God (Num 20:12 — or failing to). The Sabbath passages are highlighted — they are the only instances where the sanctification is simultaneously a named sign, an eternal covenant, and tied directly to God's identity as Sanctifier.

Sanctification & Overcoming Sin
How the Sabbath anchors the whole process of victory over sin

The Sabbath as the Weekly Re-Centering of the Sanctification Process

Sanctification is not a single event — Scripture presents it as a progressive lifelong process (Heb 10:14: "those who are being sanctified"). The Sabbath, kept every seven days, is not merely a day off — it is the recurring covenant renewal of the sanctification relationship. Every Sabbath, the believer re-encounters the God who sanctifies, re-acknowledges their dependence, re-releases the week's accumulation of worldly attachment, and is re-filled by the One whose creative and redemptive power is the only source of genuine holiness.

The link to overcoming sin is direct: sin is anything contrary to God's character. Sanctification — the process of becoming more like God — is therefore the progressive displacement of sin. You cannot be sanctified without sin being overcome. They are not two processes; they are one.

Leviticus 20:7–8
God addresses the whole nation's pattern of moral corruption
"Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be holy, for I am the Lord your God. And you shall keep My statutes, and perform them: I am the Lord who sanctifies you."
Both sides of sanctification in one sentence: human effort ("sanctify yourselves") and divine power ("I am the Lord who sanctifies you"). The human action is to keep God's statutes — the divine response is the actual transformation. Neither works without the other. EGW: "It is evident that we cannot be sanctified, or become holy, without making special efforts to overcome sin." (SLH 6.4)
John 17:17
Jesus' High Priestly prayer on the night of His arrest
"Sanctify them through Your truth. Your word is truth."
Sanctification is through truth — not ritual, not feeling, not experience alone. The word of God is the instrument through which the Spirit works transformation. The Sabbath, kept properly, is a weekly immersion in truth — in who God is, what He has done, and what He promises to do. This is why Isaiah 58:13–14 describes Sabbath-keeping in terms of delighting in God's character, not merely abstaining from work.
Ezekiel 36:26–27
God's promise of the New Covenant — the Sabbath covenant's inner fulfilment
"I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you; I will remove from you your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. And I will put My Spirit in you and move you to follow My decrees and be careful to keep My laws."
The New Covenant is not the abolition of God's statutes — it is their internalization. The stone-heart person keeps the Sabbath grudgingly or not at all. The new-heart person keeps it because the Spirit within moves them toward it. Sanctification is the Spirit writing the law — including the Sabbath law — into the character rather than merely engraving it on stone.
Hebrews 4:9–11
The NT exposition of Sabbath rest as a spiritual reality
"There remains, then, a Sabbath-rest for the people of God; for anyone who enters God's rest also rests from their works, just as God did from his. Let us, therefore, make every effort to enter that rest, so that no one will perish by following their example of disobedience."
The writer does not replace the Sabbath — he deepens it. The weekly Sabbath rest is a type of the spiritual rest of ceasing from self-effort and trusting God's work. Resting from your own works is the core posture of overcoming sin — it is the acknowledgement that only God's power can sanctify. Disobedience is the refusal to enter this rest. Sabbath is the enacted theology of grace.
Isaiah 58:13–14
God describes the Sabbath-keeper who will be lifted to the heights
"If you turn away your foot from the Sabbath, from doing your pleasure on My holy day, and call the Sabbath a delight, the holy day of the Lord honorable, and shall honor Him, not doing your own ways, nor finding your own pleasure, nor speaking your own words — then you shall delight yourself in the Lord."
The Sabbath commandment in Isaiah is a description of self-denial — "not doing your own ways, nor finding your own pleasure." This is sanctification language: the displacement of self by God. The Sabbath, rightly kept, is the weekly practice of this displacement. The promise is intimacy with God — "you shall delight yourself in the Lord." Overcoming sin is the fruit of this intimacy.
1 Thessalonians 5:23
Paul's benediction — entire-person sanctification
"Now may the God of peace Himself sanctify you completely; and may your whole spirit, soul, and body be preserved blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ."
The goal of sanctification is entire-person holiness — spirit, soul, and body — preserved to the second coming. This is not achieved in a moment; it is the lifelong trajectory of a person who repeatedly encounters the God of peace. The weekly Sabbath is the structured, covenant-sealed moment for that encounter. The Sabbath does not save — but it keeps the believer oriented toward the One who does.
✦ The Deeper Logic ✦

Why the Sabbath Specifically — and Not Just Any Day of Rest?

The question is sometimes raised: why does it matter which day, as long as there is a day of rest? The answer lies in what the Sabbath communicates. The seventh day was not chosen arbitrarily — it was the day God rested, the day He blessed, and the day He sanctified. The specific day carries the memory of creation and redemption. To keep a different day is to substitute a human institution for a divine one — and in doing so, to unknowingly detach the sign from the One the sign points to. EGW wrote: "The Sabbath is not a human institution; it is a divine institution. He who seeks to disconnect the sanctification from the day God set apart is seeking to disconnect the believer from the God who sanctifies."

Put differently: the Sabbath works as a sanctifying institution precisely because God is in it. He put His blessing and His holiness into the seventh day. Resting on another day may be a personal choice, but it does not carry the covenant weight, the Creator's rest, or the name YHWH Mekoddishkem that the seventh-day Sabbath bears.

The Sanctification Chain
How the Sabbath connects Creation → Redemption → Overcoming → Glory

The Sabbath Spans the Entire Redemptive Arc

The Sabbath is not a Sinai institution that ended at the cross. It appears at the beginning of creation (Gen 2:3), it is woven into the covenant at Sinai (Exod 20), it is the sign of sanctification in both Moses and Ezekiel (Exod 31, Ezek 20), Jesus observed it and called Himself its Lord (Luke 4:16, Mark 2:28), the New Testament deepens it (Heb 4), and it persists into the new earth (Isa 66:23). The Sabbath is the thread that runs through the entire Bible connecting God's creative power to His re-creative power in the sanctified soul.

✦ The Three Dimensions of Sabbath Sanctification ✦
Dimension 1 — Positional
The Sabbath declares that you belong to God the Sanctifier
When a person keeps the Sabbath, they are saying: "I acknowledge YHWH Mekoddishkem as my God — the One who is making me holy." This is a positional declaration — it places them in the sanctification covenant. It does not make them holy by the act itself, but it aligns them with the One who can. The Sabbath is the weekly covenant renewal of this position.
Dimension 2 — Progressive
Every Sabbath is an encounter with God that advances the transformation
EGW: "True sanctification is harmony with God, oneness with Him in character." Every Sabbath spent in genuine communion with God — in study of His word, in prayer, in nature, in fellowship — incrementally reshapes the character into alignment with His. This is the progressive dimension: not dramatic, not always felt, but real. The person who keeps 52 Sabbaths a year in genuine encounter with God is being changed across decades.
Dimension 3 — Eschatological
The Sabbath points to the final rest — complete sanctification at Christ's appearing
Hebrews 4 teaches that the weekly Sabbath rest is a type of the ultimate rest — entering fully into God's purposes. 1 Thess 5:23 describes God sanctifying the whole person "at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ." Rev 22:11: "Let him who is holy be holy still." The Sabbath is the weekly rehearsal for that final state. Those who have weekly practiced rest in God, dependence on God, and delight in God will be prepared for the eternal Sabbath of the new earth.

Summary: What the Sabbath Does in the Sanctification Process

1. It re-declares God's identity as Sanctifier — YHWH Mekoddishkem is not a title for Sundays; it is attached to the Sabbath by covenant.

2. It re-establishes the believer's dependence — ceasing from work is ceasing from self-reliance. Sabbath rest is enacted trust.

3. It provides the space for genuine encounter — sanctification comes through truth (John 17:17) and through the Spirit (2 Thess 2:13). The Sabbath is the covenant-sealed, weekly-protected time for both.

4. It disciplines the will — keeping the Sabbath against the pressure of worldly obligation, business, or entertainment is the weekly exercise of choosing God over comfort. EGW: "He who from the heart obeys the fourth commandment will obey the whole law."

5. It is the sign that overcomes — in the final conflict, the Sabbath will be the test of loyalty. Those who have been sanctified through Sabbath-keeping will stand when the world chooses the counterfeit. The mark of the beast is the counterfeit of the seal of God — and the seal of God is His name written in the character, of which the Sabbath is the sign.

Ellen G. White — The Great Controversy, p. 605
"The seal of the living God… is placed upon those who keep the commandments of God and have the faith of Jesus. Those who receive the seal must be sanctified through the truth; their minds must be stayed on God; their wills surrendered to Him. The Sabbath is the test of loyalty — for it is the point of truth especially controverted. While the observance of the false Sabbath in compliance with the Sunday law will be an avowal of allegiance to a power that is in opposition to God, the keeping of the true Sabbath will be the evidence of loyalty to the Creator."
The Great Controversy, p. 605 (paraphrase)
