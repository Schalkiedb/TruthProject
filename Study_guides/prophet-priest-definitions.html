What the Bible Actually Means

Prophet · Priest · The Roles Most People Misunderstand

Most people think a prophet predicts the future. Most people think a priest performs religious rituals. Both assumptions are dangerously incomplete — and both misunderstandings have enormous consequences for how we identify false prophets, evaluate teachers, and understand the finished work of Christ. This guide corrects both definitions from Scripture itself.

Part One

The Prophet — Spokesman, Not Soothsayer

The modern reduction of "prophet" to "someone who predicts the future" is a post-classical mistake that has almost no basis in the Hebrew or Greek originals. Recovering the true definition changes everything — including who qualifies as a false prophet.

The Primary Hebrew Word
Hebrew
נָבִיא — nābî
The foundational Old Testament word for prophet — "spokesman"
+
נָבִיא
nābî / nabi
H5030 · used 316 times in the OT

Root meaning: Most scholars derive nābî from the root nāba — "to bubble forth like a fountain, to pour out speech, to utter." The emphasis is on the speaking forth of a message, not on the prediction of future events. The word fundamentally denotes a spokesman — one who speaks for another.

The defining proof text: Exodus 7:1 — "And the LORD said unto Moses, See, I have made thee a god to Pharaoh: and Aaron thy brother shall be thy prophet [nābî]." Aaron had no predictive role here. He was Moses' mouthpiece. This is the purest biblical definition: a prophet is one who speaks for another.

The Septuagint (LXX) — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Old Testament produced c. 300 BC — consistently renders nābî as προφήτης (prophētēs), a Greek word meaning one who speaks in behalf of another, especially one who speaks for a god and interprets his will. Note: the English sense "one who predicts" is a post-classical narrowing. The biblical word has always meant interpreter and spokesman first.

"And the LORD said unto Moses, See, I have made thee a god to Pharaoh: and Aaron thy brother shall be thy prophet [nābî]. Thou shalt speak all that I command thee: and Aaron thy brother shall speak unto Pharaoh." — Exodus 7:1–2, KJV (Aaron's role as prophet = Moses' spokesman)
The Definitive Proof Aaron is explicitly called Moses' nābî — prophet. His only function in this role was to repeat what Moses told him. He predicted nothing. He performed no miracles in this capacity. He was a spokesman. This single verse permanently anchors the meaning: a prophet is a commissioned spokesperson for a higher authority.

Secondary Hebrew words for prophet reinforce this. Rō'eh (H7203) — seer — emphasises the reception of the message (seeing/receiving). Ḥōzeh (H2374) — also seer — emphasises prophetic vision. All three words converge on the same function: receiving a message from God and communicating it to people. Prediction is one possible content of that message, but it is never the definition of the role itself. 1 Chronicles 29:29 uses all three in one verse: "Samuel the seer (rō'eh), Nathan the prophet (nābî), Gad the seer (ḥōzeh)."

The New Testament Greek Word
Greek
προφήτης — prophētēs
One who speaks forth for another — not primarily a prediction-maker
+
προφήτης
prophētēs
G4396 · 144 NT occurrences

Compound: pro (before, on behalf of) + phēmi (to speak). In classical Greek literature, the prophētēs at Delphi was the temple official who interpreted and proclaimed the oracle's utterances to the public — not the one who predicted them. The role was that of a communicator and interpreter, not a futurist.

The "pro" prefix: Most people assume pro means "before" (in time) as in "prophecy = speaking before the event." But in classical and biblical Greek, pro more fundamentally means in front of, on behalf of, forth. The prophet speaks forth — publicly, openly, on behalf of God — as much as he speaks before.

Scholar George Henry Phillips (1921) summarised the consensus of biblical scholarship: "Prediction may be an aspect of the prophet's message, but it is no part of the meaning of the word either in Greek or Hebrew. The prophet is not characteristically one who foretells the future: he forthtells the divine word. His task is to interpret the present, under guidance of the Spirit of God which possesses him. He hears and speaks — that is his function."

Forth-Telling and Fore-Telling — The Distinction That Changes Everything A prophet does two things that are often confused as one: (1) Forth-telling — proclaiming God's revealed will to people in their present situation: preaching, teaching, exhortation, rebuke, instruction in righteousness. (2) Fore-telling — announcing what God will do in the future. Both are prophetic functions. But forth-telling is the primary role; fore-telling is the exceptional one. Most of what Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel wrote was forth-telling — moral, theological, and covenantal proclamation. Their predictive sections are the minority, not the majority, of their total output.

The Full Prophetic Spectrum in Scripture
Biblical Range
Old & New Testament
Every type of person called a prophet — from pastors to preachers to miracle-workers
+

The Bible applies the word "prophet" (nābî / prophētēs) to a remarkably diverse range of people and functions. Reducing it to "predictor of the future" erases most of this diversity.

The Prophetic Spectrum — Biblical Examples
Lawgiver-Prophet
Deuteronomy 18:15; Acts 3:22
Moses — the supreme nābî. His primary role was receiving the law from God and teaching it to Israel. The majority of the Pentateuch is law, instruction, and historical narrative — not prediction.
Preacher-Prophet
1 Samuel 7:2–17; 3:1–21
Samuel — his primary prophetic function was preaching true religion, rebuking apostasy, instructing Israel in covenantal faithfulness. He served as teacher, judge, and guardian of the law of God.
Pastoral-Prophet
Ezekiel 3:17; 33:7–9
Ezekiel was explicitly called a "watchman" (ṣōpeh) over Israel — a shepherd-guardian function. His role was to warn, teach, and guide the flock of God. This is the same role as a pastor.
Preaching-Prophet
Jonah 3:4; Jeremiah 1:9–10
Jonah's entire message was eight Hebrew words — essentially a sermon. Jeremiah was called to "root out, pull down, destroy, build, plant" — the language of pastoral ministry over a nation.
NT Teaching-Prophet
Acts 13:1; 15:32
In Antioch there were "prophets and teachers" — listed together. Judas and Silas are called prophets (15:32) and their entire ministry was exhortation and strengthening — what we would call pastoral preaching.
Congregational Prophet
1 Corinthians 14:3, 31
Paul says "everyone can prophesy" (14:31) for "edification, exhortation, and comfort" (14:3). This congregational prophecy is not prediction — it is Spirit-directed communication of God's word for the building up of the body.
Apostle-Prophet
Ephesians 2:20; 4:11
Paul lists five ministry offices: apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers. These overlap — every apostle proclaimed God's word (prophetic function). The categories describe emphasis and calling, not watertight compartments.
Predictive-Prophet
Isaiah 53; Daniel 7–12; Revelation
The predictive dimension is real and essential — but it is one function within the broader prophetic calling, not its definition. Daniel, Isaiah, and John of Patmos were all primarily forth-tellers who also received fore-telling visions.
The Implication for False Prophets If a prophet is primarily a spokesperson for God — a teacher, preacher, or proclaimer of His will — then a false prophet is primarily a false spokesperson. That includes every pastor, teacher, or preacher who proclaims a message that is not from God. You do not have to predict the future to be a false prophet. You simply have to claim to speak for God and speak something different from what God says. This is exactly why Jeremiah 23 describes false prophets as men who "speak visions of their own heart" — they are forth-tellers of a false message, not failed fore-tellers.

τοὺς δὲ ποιμένας καὶ διδασκάλους
tous de poimenas kai didaskalous
G4166 + G1320 · "the pastors and teachers"

Grammatical note: In Greek, pastors and teachers share a single definite article (tous) — a construction called the Granville Sharp Rule — strongly suggesting these two offices are aspects of one ministry. The pastor-teacher is a single figure with two dimensions: shepherding the flock (pastoral) and instructing them in truth (teaching). This is the same dual function as the Old Testament prophet-teacher.

Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 12:28 confirms the parallel: "God has appointed in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers." In that list, prophets and teachers are adjacent and functionally overlapping. A prophet teaches; a teacher prophesies. Both speak forth the word of God to the congregation. The distinction is one of emphasis and gift — both roles are prophetic in the original biblical sense.

Every person in this list is a spokesperson for God — which means every person in this list operates under prophetic accountability. The same tests that apply to a predictive prophet (Isaiah 8:20, Deuteronomy 13, Matthew 7:20) apply with equal force to the pastor who teaches his congregation every Sabbath. He is a nābî — a spokesman for God. If he speaks false doctrine, leads people away from God's commandments, or preaches a message of his own heart, he is a false prophet regardless of whether he has ever predicted anything.

"Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers, for you know that we who teach will be judged with greater strictness." (James 3:1) The teacher who stands before a congregation wields prophetic authority and bears prophetic accountability. Ellen White repeatedly warned that the pastoral office must be exercised with trembling: 'The greatest want of the world is the want of men... men who will not be bought or sold; men who in their inmost souls are true and honest; men who do not fear to call sin by its right name.'"

Part Two

The Priest — A Role That Has Been Permanently Transferred

The Levitical priesthood was God-ordained. It was also deliberately temporary — a shadow of the true. At the cross, the veil was torn, the sacrifices ended, and the entire priestly system was superseded. Any earthly figure who claims priestly mediatory authority — the ability to forgive sins, offer sacrifice, or stand between God and man — is claiming a role that belongs exclusively and permanently to Jesus Christ.

The Levitical Priest's Functions
  • Offered animal sacrifices for sin (Lev. 4–5)
  • Performed daily rituals in the sanctuary (Heb. 9:6)
  • Entered the Holy of Holies once a year — Day of Atonement (Lev. 16; Heb. 9:7)

  • Christ's once-for-all sacrifice at Calvary (Heb. 9:26)
  • Christ's continuous intercession in heaven (Heb. 7:25)
  • Christ entering the heavenly Holy of Holies with His own blood (Heb. 9:12)
  • Christ: the one Mediator between God and man (1 Tim. 2:5)
  • Christ the Word teaching directly through His Spirit (John 14:26)
  • Christ the one who declares righteous (Rom. 8:33–34)
  • Christ the one through whom all blessing comes (Eph. 1:3)

Every single function of the Levitical priesthood was a shadow of what Christ would accomplish perfectly. The writer of Hebrews makes this the centrepiece of his entire argument (chapters 7–10): the earthly sanctuary was "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (8:5). The priests who served it were temporal, replaceable, and unable to fully atone — they were signposts, not the destination.

ἀθέτησις
athetēsis
G115 — Hebrews 7:18

"Disannulling" (KJV) / "Setting aside" — athetēsis (G115) is a legal term meaning annulment, abrogation, the formal setting aside of a prior arrangement. It is used in legal documents of the first century for the cancellation of a contract. Hebrews 7:18 uses it for the annulment of the former commandment (the Levitical law governing the priesthood): "For there is verily a disannulling of the commandment going before for the weakness and unprofitableness thereof."

This is not a soft transition or a gradual phase-out. The legal force of athetēsis is a permanent cancellation. The Levitical priesthood — with its sacrifices, its rituals, its incense, its intercessory function — was legally annulled when Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary with His own blood. No human being inherits this role. No succession is possible or valid.

⚠ The Torn Veil — God's Own Act of Abolition Matthew 27:51: "And, behold, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom." The direction of the tearing is significant — from top to bottom means God tore it, not man. The veil that separated the Holy of Holies — the space only the high priest could enter, once a year, under strict ceremony — was destroyed by God Himself at the moment of Christ's death. The message was unmistakable: the earthly sanctuary system was finished. Direct access to God was now open to every believer through Christ alone (Heb. 10:19–22).
"Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh... Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith." — Hebrews 10:19–20, 22, KJV

Hebrews 8:4 goes further: "For if he were on earth, he should not be a priest." Christ Himself cannot exercise His priesthood on earth — it is an exclusively heavenly ministry. Any earthly figure who claims priestly authority — the power to forgive sins through sacramental action, to offer sacrifice, to act as mediator between a person and God — is not just mistaken. They are claiming a vacancy that does not exist and a transfer of authority that has not occurred.

ἀπαράβατον
aparabaton
G531 — Hebrews 7:24

"Unchangeable" — aparabatos (G531) is a legal term used in ancient documents meaning not passing to a successor, inviolable, intransmissible. It describes a priesthood that cannot be transferred — the opposite of the Levitical priesthood where the office passed from father to son, generation to generation.

Aparabatos appears only once in the entire New Testament — here. God chose the strongest possible legal language to declare: Christ's priesthood cannot pass to anyone else. It is held by Him alone, permanently, without the possibility of succession. There is no apostolic succession into the priestly office. There is no human being, however ordained, who shares in Christ's mediatory priesthood. The gap between God and man has one bridge — Jesus Christ. And that bridge is permanently held by One who "always lives to make intercession" (v.25).

1 Timothy 2:5 — The Final Statement "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." The word mesitēs (G3316) — mediator — is a legal term for the one who stands between two parties to broker a covenant. Paul does not say "one primary mediator" or "one high mediator with human sub-mediators." He says one. The definitive article and the numeral together function as an absolute exclusion. Anyone who claims to stand between you and God in a mediatory-priestly capacity — priest, confessor, intercessor by title — is claiming the office of Christ. That office is not available.

Pulling It Together — Implications for the False Prophet Tests

What These Definitions Change

Once the true definitions of prophet and priest are in place, the implications for identifying false prophets — and for the topic of obedience — become significantly sharper.

Implication 1 — Every Pastor Is a Prophet
Every person who stands before a congregation and claims to speak God's word is functioning as a nābî — a spokesman for God. The Deuteronomy 13 test, the Isaiah 8:20 test, and the Matthew 7:20 fruit test apply to him in full. He does not need to have made a prediction. He only needs to be claiming to represent God's message. If that message contradicts God's commandments, he is a false prophet by the biblical definition.
Implication 2 — "I'm Not a Prophet, I'm a Teacher" Is No Excuse
In Scripture these functions overlap. Teaching is a prophetic act — it is forth-telling the will of God. A teacher who leads people away from God's law is operating as a false prophet whether or not he claims the prophetic title. Jeremiah 23's false prophets did not all call themselves prophets — some were priests and teachers (v.11, "both prophet and priest are profane"). The category is functional, not merely titular.
Implication 3 — No Human Is a Priest in the Mediatory Sense
The Levitical priesthood — sacrifice, mediation, intercessory standing before God on behalf of sinners — was abolished at the cross and transferred exclusively to Christ. Any system that requires a human priest between a believer and God (for confession, absolution, sacramental grace) is claiming a vacancy that Christ has permanently filled. The torn veil made this architectural: no more human gatekeeper to the presence of God.
Implication 4 — "Priest" vs. "Prophet" Is a False Escape
Arguing that someone is "just a priest, not a prophet" — as if this avoids the false prophet tests — misunderstands both roles. Jeremiah 23:11 explicitly says "both prophet and priest are profane" — God makes no distinction in his indictment. And Malachi 2:7–8 condemns priests who "caused many to stumble at the law" as having "corrupted the covenant of Levi." The accountability is identical.
Implication 5 — Obedience and the Teaching Office
Since every teacher is a prophetic spokesman, the command of Revelation 14:12 — keeping the commandments of God — applies to what teachers proclaim. A teacher who preaches that God's commandments are optional is not just theologically mistaken. He is a false prophet by Deuteronomy 13: he is leading people away from the law of God, regardless of whether his signs come true. The two studies (obedience and false prophets) are not separate topics — they are two sides of the same coin.
Implication 6 — The Universal Priesthood of Believers
1 Peter 2:9 calls all believers "a royal priesthood" — but the context defines this as declaring God's praise and representing His character to the world, not mediating between sinners and God. Revelation 1:6 similarly says Christ has made us "kings and priests." This is the prophetic-pastoral function: every believer is called to proclaim and live the gospel. It is the forth-telling dimension of priesthood — not the sin-mediating dimension, which belongs to Christ alone.
