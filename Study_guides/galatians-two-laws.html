Galatians & the Two Laws

The Ceremonial Law, the Moral Law, and the Gospel of Grace
Paul's letter to the Galatians targets the Mosaic ceremonial ordinances imposed as conditions of salvation — not the eternal moral law of God. Understanding this distinction is not a fine theological point: it is the difference between the true gospel and a counterfeit one.

I · Background

The Crisis in Galatia

The letter to the Galatians was written by Paul in response to a specific, urgent crisis: Judaizing teachers had followed his missionary work and were insisting that Gentile believers must submit to circumcision and adopt the full apparatus of Mosaic covenant observance in order to be truly saved. This was not an abstract theological debate — it was a direct attack on the sufficiency of Christ.

Paul calls it "another gospel" (Gal. 1:6–7) and responds with his most passionate and personal letter. The entire argument of Galatians is structured around one question: On what basis does a sinner stand righteous before God?

⚠ The Specific Threat

The Judaizers were not teaching that the moral law saves. They were insisting that circumcision and Mosaic covenant identity markers were prerequisites for salvation alongside faith in Christ — effectively making Moses a co-mediator with Jesus.

Understanding this historical situation is essential. Paul wrote to this crisis in this church. His polemic is a precision instrument aimed at the Mosaic ceremonial system as a mechanism of justification — not a broadside against God's eternal moral character enshrined in the Decalogue.

✦
II · Foundation

Distinguishing the Two Laws

Scripture itself presents two distinct bodies of law operating in Israel's covenant life. Confusing them is the root error that leads to either legalism (burdening conscience with ceremonial obligations) or antinomianism (abolishing God's moral standard). Both are errors the biblical authors explicitly resist.

Feature The Ceremonial / Mosaic Law The Moral Law (Decalogue)
Origin Given through Moses at Sinai Spoken by God's own voice; written by His finger
Inscribed on Parchment scrolls (the "book of the law") Two tables of stone — imperishable material
Location in the sanctuary Placed beside (outside) the ark Placed inside the ark of the covenant
Character Shadows and types pointing to Christ Reflection of God's eternal character
Duration Until "the seed" (Christ) came — Gal. 3:19 "Forever and ever" — Psalm 111:7–8; Matt. 5:18
Nailed to the cross? Yes — Col. 2:14 (the certificate of debt) No — its penalty was paid, not its obligation removed
Includes Circumcision, feast days, sacrifices, dietary identity markers, Levitical priesthood Ten Commandments including the Sabbath of the fourth
Paul's verdict in Galatians Cannot justify; was a guardian until Christ Not addressed as abolished — upheld through love
✦ Key Distinction

The ceremonial law was added "because of transgressions" (Gal. 3:19) — it was a temporary, remedial system pointing forward to Christ. The moral law was not added; it existed before Sinai (Romans 5:13–14 presupposes it), and its binding character is never treated as contingent on the Mosaic covenant.

III · The Argument

What Paul Is Actually Targeting in Galatians

A careful reading of Galatians reveals that Paul's specific targets are the identity markers of the Mosaic covenant — the works that separated Jew from Gentile and which the Judaizers were imposing as requirements for full standing before God.

Circumcision — The Central Issue

Circumcision is mentioned explicitly in Gal. 2:3, 2:7–9, 2:12, 5:2–3, 5:6, 5:11, 6:12–13, 6:15. It was the entry rite into the Mosaic covenant and the specific demand of the Judaizers. Paul's argument in 5:3 is decisive:

Galatians 5:2–3 · KJV
"Behold, I Paul say unto you, that if ye be circumcised, Christ shall profit you nothing. For I testify again to every man that is circumcised, that he is a debtor to do the whole law."
Accepting circumcision as a salvific act places a person under obligation to the entire Mosaic covenant system — an impossible burden Paul refuses to reimpose.

The Mosaic Calendar — Days, Months, Times, Years

Galatians 4:10 is frequently misapplied to the weekly Sabbath. But the context — Paul comparing Mosaic observance to pagan elemental worship (4:8–9) — makes clear he is addressing the Mosaic feast cycle: new moon observances, annual feast days, sabbatical years, and jubilee cycles. These were shadows (Col. 2:17) of the redemptive calendar pointing to Christ.

Galatians 4:10 · KJV
"Ye observe days, and months, and times, and years."
The plural "days" matches the Mosaic feast calendar (Lev. 23), not the singular weekly creation Sabbath rooted in Genesis 2:1–3.
Colossians 2:16–17 · KJV
"Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ."
The "sabbath days" here are the annual ceremonial sabbaths of the Mosaic calendar — not the weekly Sabbath of the Decalogue.
Exegetical Note

The weekly Sabbath was instituted at creation (Gen. 2:1–3) — two millennia before Moses, before Israel, before any ceremonial system. It belongs to the created order itself, not to the Mosaic covenant. To abolish it from Galatians is to read a post-Sinai dispute about circumcision back into a pre-Sinai creation ordinance.

IV · Exegesis

Key Passages in Galatians — Examined

Galatians 3:10–13 — The Curse of the Law
Galatians 3:10, 13 · KJV
"For as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse: for it is written, Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them... Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us."

Paul quotes Deuteronomy 27:26 — explicitly from the Mosaic covenant curse framework, not from the Decalogue. The "book of the law" is the Mosaic code written by Moses and placed beside the ark (Deut. 31:26), distinct from the tablets of stone within it. Christ redeemed us from the condemnation of the law — its penal consequences — not from its moral authority.

Galatians 3:19 — Why Was the Law Added?
Galatians 3:19 · KJV
"Wherefore then serveth the law? It was added because of transgressions, till the seed should come to whom the promise was made."

The phrase "it was added" (Greek: prosetethē) indicates something appended to a pre-existing covenant — the Abrahamic promise. The moral law was not "added" to anything; it predates Sinai and is intrinsic to God's character. The Mosaic ceremonial system, however, was "added" as a temporary remedial scaffold until "the seed" — Christ — arrived. Its temporal limitation ("till the seed should come") is explicit.

Galatians 3:24–25 — The Schoolmaster / Guardian
Galatians 3:24–25 · KJV
"Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith. But after that faith is come, we are no longer under a schoolmaster."

The Greek word is paidagōgos — a household slave who escorted children to school and maintained discipline, not a teacher. The ceremonial law functioned exactly this way: its sacrifices, priesthood, and rituals pointed Israel toward the coming Messiah. Once Christ arrived, the guardian's task was complete. This does not eliminate the moral law — it fulfills what the guardian was pointing to.

Galatians 5:14 — The Law Fulfilled Through Love
Galatians 5:14 · KJV
"For all the law is fulfilled in this; Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself."

Having dismantled the ceremonial law as a justification mechanism, Paul then affirms love as the fulfillment of the moral law's second table. This is a direct echo of Leviticus 19:18 and Jesus' teaching in Matthew 22:37–40. Love does not abolish the Decalogue — it is the Decalogue lived from the inside. Paul is not saying "love replaces law"; he is saying love performs the law's deepest intent.

Galatians 6:2 — The Law of Christ
Galatians 6:2 · KJV
"Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ."

Paul closes by referencing "the law of Christ" — a law that is to be fulfilled, not abolished. This is the moral law internalized through the Spirit, consistent with the new covenant promise in Jeremiah 31:33: "I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts." The contrast with the Mosaic system is not law versus no-law; it is external ordinance versus internalized character transformation.

V · Colossians 2:14

The Handwriting of Ordinances

Colossians 2:14 runs as the essential parallel to Galatians and is often misread to mean the Decalogue was nailed to the cross. A precise Greek word study demolishes this interpretation.

Colossians 2:14 · KJV
"Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross."
χειρόγραφον
cheirographon · "handwriting"
A handwritten certificate of debt — a legal IOU. In the Greco-Roman world this was a bond signed by the debtor acknowledging obligation. Paul's imagery: the Mosaic covenant certificate of debt accumulated our transgressions against us. Christ cancelled that debt instrument at the cross.

This word is never used of the Decalogue in any biblical or extra-biblical literature. The Ten Commandments were not a handwritten ordinance — they were inscribed in stone by the finger of God, kept inside the ark, and constitute God's character rather than a debt instrument. What was "against us" was the record of our transgressions measured by the law — not the law itself.

✦ The Distinction in Stone vs. Scroll

Deuteronomy 31:26 records Moses placing the book of the law beside the ark — a witness against Israel. The Decalogue was placed inside the ark (Deut. 10:5) — under the mercy seat, covered by atonement. This spatial distinction in the sanctuary encodes the theological difference between conditional Mosaic ordinances and God's eternal moral character.

✦
VI · The Decalogue

The Moral Law Upheld — Not Abolished

Paul's most explicit statement on the moral law comes not in Galatians but in Romans — written to address the same antinomian misreading that Galatians might produce.

Romans 3:31 · KJV
"Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law."
Paul pre-empts the antinomian misreading of his own gospel: faith establishes the law — it places the law where it belongs, as a standard of righteousness fulfilled by Christ and written on the believer's heart.
Romans 7:12 · KJV
"Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good."
Paul never describes the Decalogue as a burden, an enemy, or an obsolete system. His language is uniformly reverential: holy, just, good.
Romans 7:22 · KJV
"For I delight in the law of God after the inward man."
The regenerate heart delights in the law — echoing Psalm 119:97. This is not the language of someone describing an abolished system.
Matthew 5:17–18 · KJV
"Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law."
Christ Himself settles the question. "Heaven and earth" have not passed. The moral law stands.
Ellen G. White · The Great Controversy
"The law of God, as presented in the Scriptures, is the only standard of righteousness. It is the expression of the divine will — a transcript of God's character... The moral law never has been done away; it will never be done away. It is the foundation of God's government in heaven and earth."
— Ellen G. White, The Great Controversy, p. 467

VII · The Gospel Order

The Chain: Justification → Sanctification → Obedience

The proper relationship between faith, grace, and law-keeping is not the abolition of law but its relocation — from a tablet of stone to a heart of flesh (Jer. 31:33; Ezek. 36:26–27). The sequence is critical:

1
Recognition of Sin Through the Law
The moral law reveals the standard — "I had not known sin, but by the law" (Rom. 7:7). The law is a diagnostic, not a cure.
2
Justification by Faith in Christ
The sinner, convicted, turns to Christ alone for righteousness. "Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Rom. 3:24). No works contribute to this standing.
3
Regeneration by the Holy Spirit
The Spirit births a new nature — "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3). The desires change at the root.
4
Sanctification — Growth in Holiness
The justified, regenerate believer grows in conformity to Christ's character — a lifelong process. "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God which worketh in you" (Phil. 2:12–13).
5
Obedience as the Fruit of Love
The regenerate heart obeys because it loves — "If ye love me, keep my commandments" (John 14:15). Obedience is not the root of standing but the fruit of it. Law-keeping is the evidence, not the engine, of salvation.
✦ The Critical Distinction

Legalism says: I keep the law to earn God's approval.
Antinomianism says: Grace has made the law irrelevant.
The Gospel says: I am accepted freely by grace, and therefore I keep the law joyfully — not to secure acceptance, but because I am already accepted, and the law reflects the character of the God I love.

Ellen G. White · Steps to Christ
"The condition of eternal life is now just what it always has been — just what it was in Paradise before the fall of our first parents — perfect righteousness, harmony with God, perfect conformity to the principles of His law... Instead of releasing man from obedience, it is faith, and faith only, that makes us partakers of the grace of Christ, which enables us to render obedience."
— Ellen G. White, Steps to Christ, p. 62

VIII · Errors to Avoid

Common Misreadings of Galatians

Galatians is one of the most misapplied books in the New Testament. The following errors follow directly from failing to distinguish the two laws:

Error 1: "Galatians abolishes the Sabbath"

This reads Galatians 4:10 ("Ye observe days...") as a prohibition against Sabbath-keeping. But the Sabbath is a creation ordinance (Gen. 2:1–3) — it precedes Moses, Israel, and the Sinai covenant by two thousand years. The Sabbath belongs to the Decalogue written by God's finger; Paul's target in Galatians is circumcision and Mosaic feast-day observance as salvific requirements. To extend Paul's argument to the weekly Sabbath is to misread his precision argument as a blunt instrument against the entire law of God.

Error 2: "Paul's 'works of the law' means the Ten Commandments"

The Greek phrase erga nomou ("works of the law") in Galatians consistently refers to Mosaic covenant performance — specifically circumcision and the boundary markers that distinguished Jew from Gentile. Modern New Perspective scholars (Dunn, Wright) have helpfully clarified this. Paul is not attacking moral effort per se; he is attacking the Judaizing system that made Mosaic boundary markers the gateway into the covenant community.

Error 3: "We are not under law but under grace — therefore the law doesn't apply"

Romans 6:14–15 reads: "Ye are not under the law, but under grace. What then? Shall we sin because we are not under grace? God forbid." Paul himself identifies this reasoning as a distortion. "Not under law" means not under the law's condemning power — not exempt from its moral claim. The believer has been freed from the penalty of law-breaking, not from the law's standard. Grace produces obedience; it does not make obedience optional.

Error 4: "Galatians 3:28 means all distinctions — including Sabbath vs. Sunday — are erased"

Galatians 3:28 ("There is neither Jew nor Greek...") addresses standing before God in justification — all are equally accepted in Christ, regardless of ethnic or social status. It says nothing about the calendar, the Sabbath, or the Decalogue. This is a soteriological statement, not a liturgical one. Applying it to abolish time-based creation ordinances is a category error.

IX · Spirit of Prophecy

Ellen G. White on Galatians and the Two Laws

EGW's treatment of Galatians is extensive and consistent. She identified the "law in Galatians" question as one of the most important theological distinctions for the remnant church to understand clearly.

Ellen G. White · Selected Messages, Book 1
"The law of ten commandments is not to be looked upon as much from the prohibitory side as from the mercy side. Its prohibitions are the sure guarantee of happiness in obedience. As received in Christ, it works in us the purity of character that will bring joy to us through eternal ages. To the obedient it is a wall of protection."
— Ellen G. White, Selected Messages, Book 1, p. 235

"There are two distinct laws brought to view in the Scriptures. One is the law of ceremonies and types which pointed to Christ as the Saviour of the world and was done away at His death. The other is the moral law — the Ten Commandments — which is the transcript of God's character and will stand throughout eternity."
— Ellen G. White, Patriarchs and Prophets, p. 365

These ceremonies were abolished at the cross. But the moral law, given in Eden and proclaimed from Sinai, was not nailed to the cross."
— Ellen G. White, Review and Herald, September 1, 1896

X · Conclusion

Summary — What Galatians Teaches

✓ What Paul Abolishes
The Mosaic ceremonial law as a mechanism of justification — circumcision, feast days, annual sabbaths, sacrificial ordinances, and all Mosaic covenant identity markers imposed as salvific requirements.
✓ What Paul Upholds
The eternal moral law — the Decalogue — as the standard of righteousness, fulfilled inwardly through the Spirit, expressed outwardly through love, and kept joyfully as the fruit of justifying faith.
✗ What Paul Does NOT Teach
That God's moral law is abolished, that the Sabbath is obsolete, that obedience is optional, or that grace removes all ethical obligation from the believer.

The letter to the Galatians is one of the most powerful defenses of the gospel ever written. Its power lies precisely in its precision: Paul dismantles the ceremonial law as a justification mechanism while leaving the moral law standing as God's eternal standard — now written on the heart by the Spirit rather than imposed from outside by external ordinances.

We are not saved by keeping the moral law. But the saved keep the moral law — not to earn standing before God, but because they have been given new hearts that love what God loves. As John declared: "For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous" (1 John 5:3).

Ellen G. White · Christ's Object Lessons
"Obedience — the service and allegiance of love — is the true sign of discipleship. Thus the Scripture says, 'This is the love of God, that we keep his commandments.' Instead of releasing man from obedience, it is faith, and faith only, that makes us partakers of the grace of Christ, which enables us to render obedience."
— Ellen G. White, Christ's Object Lessons, p. 97
