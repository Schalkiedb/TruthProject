Part One — The Foundational Discovery

The Warburg Effect — Cancer Runs on Sugar

In 1931, German biochemist Otto Warburg won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering what is now called the Warburg Effect: cancer cells consume glucose at a rate 10–70 times higher than normal cells, even when oxygen is available. Normal cells produce energy efficiently through oxidative phosphorylation. Cancer cells abandon this efficient pathway and instead ferment glucose — a far less efficient process — at extraordinary speed. The question of why they do this is still debated. The implication of what it means for diet is not.

Why Do Cancer Cells Prefer Glucose?

The Warburg Effect is not simply a malfunction. Cancer cells upregulate glucose transporters (GLUT1, GLUT3) on their surface — actively pulling glucose from surrounding tissue. They also overexpress the enzymes of glycolysis and downregulate the mitochondrial pathways that normal cells use. This metabolic reprogramming serves cancer's goals precisely:

  • Rapid energy production — fermentation is inefficient per glucose molecule but can run at extraordinary speed when glucose is abundant, sustaining rapid cell division that normal cells cannot match
  • Biosynthetic raw materials — glycolytic intermediates are diverted into the synthesis of the amino acids, lipids, and nucleotides needed to build new cancer cells
  • Acidic microenvironment — lactic acid produced by fermentation lowers the pH around the tumour. This acidic environment destroys surrounding healthy tissue, creates pathways for invasion, and suppresses immune cells that might otherwise attack the tumour
  • Hypoxia survival — unlike normal cells that die without oxygen, cancer cells can survive and grow in low-oxygen environments using fermentation — which is why solid tumours can grow larger than their blood supply would normally support

Research linking insulin to cancer outcomes found that[3] elevated fasting insulin — produced in direct response to dietary sugar and refined carbohydrates — was independently associated with cancer progression, recurrence, and mortality across multiple cancer types. Insulin does not merely correlate with cancer outcomes — it activates the same growth signalling pathways (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) that cancer cells use to drive proliferation.

PET Scans — Medicine Already Knows Tumours Love Sugar

When oncologists want to find cancer in the body, they inject radioactive glucose (18F-fluorodeoxyglucose) and scan where it concentrates. Tumours light up intensely because they absorb glucose far faster than surrounding tissue.[2] The entire standard-of-care cancer detection methodology is built on the Warburg Effect. Medicine has known for decades that cancer consumes glucose abnormally — yet dietary sugar reduction remains almost entirely absent from mainstream oncology treatment protocols.

Part Two — The Craving Question

Does Cancer Make a Person Crave Sugar More?

This is one of the most important and least-discussed questions in cancer nutrition. The answer from emerging research is: yes — through multiple measurable mechanisms. Cancer does not merely passively consume available glucose. It actively manipulates the host's hormonal, neurological, and metabolic systems to increase glucose availability. This is a survival strategy that has profound practical implications for dietary management.

Mechanism 1 — Tumour-Driven Insulin Resistance

Tumours secrete cytokines and metabolic signals that promote systemic insulin resistance in surrounding tissues. When normal cells become insulin resistant, glucose is no longer efficiently taken up by muscle, fat, and liver cells — leaving more glucose circulating in the bloodstream, available to the tumour's glucose-hungry cells. This is not coincidental. Research published in Cancer Research (2014) documented that pancreatic cancer cells actively promote systemic insulin resistance to increase circulating glucose availability for their own consumption.

📊 Research — Nature (2019) — Tumour Metabolic Reprogramming

Tumour cells compete directly with the brain for circulating glucose. When tumour glucose demand is high, the brain experiences relative glucose deprivation[4] — triggering the hypothalamus to increase hunger signals and specifically amplify carbohydrate cravings. This is a documented neuroendocrine response: the tumour effectively hijacks the body's food-seeking behaviour to ensure its own glucose supply.

Mechanism 2 — Cachexia and Metabolic Reprogramming

Cancer cachexia — the severe wasting syndrome that affects 50–80% of cancer patients — involves massive metabolic reprogramming. The body begins breaking down muscle protein and fat at accelerated rates. This produces glucose through gluconeogenesis (conversion of protein to glucose) — maintaining high circulating glucose even when dietary intake is reduced. The Cori cycle, by which tumour-produced lactate is converted back to glucose in the liver at significant metabolic cost, runs continuously — consuming 40% of total energy expenditure in some advanced cancer cases.

Mechanism 3 — Inflammatory Cytokines Drive Carbohydrate Appetite

Tumours produce IL-6, TNF-alpha, and other inflammatory cytokines as part of their immune evasion strategy. These same cytokines cross the blood-brain barrier and directly alter hypothalamic appetite regulation — specifically increasing preference for high-glycaemic foods. Research in the journal Brain, Behavior, and Immunity (2013) confirmed that cytokine-driven appetite changes in cancer patients produce measurably higher carbohydrate cravings independent of caloric needs.[5]

Mechanism 4 — Serotonin Depletion and Sweet Cravings

Tumours frequently disrupt gut microbiome composition — reducing the beneficial bacteria responsible for serotonin production. Low serotonin is one of the most powerful drivers of sugar and carbohydrate cravings (serotonin is synthesised from tryptophan via a pathway that requires carbohydrate-driven insulin to facilitate tryptophan uptake into the brain). A tumour-compromised gut produces less serotonin → more carbohydrate craving → more glucose → faster tumour growth. This represents a precise, self-reinforcing metabolic loop that cancer exploits.

⚠ The Practical Implication

When a cancer patient experiences intense sugar and carbohydrate cravings — which is extremely common — this is not weakness of will. It is the tumour's survival strategy expressing itself through the patient's appetite. Understanding this changes how dietary guidance during cancer treatment should be framed: it is not about restricting what the patient wants. It is about recognising that what the patient intensely craves may be precisely what the tumour most needs.

Insulin is not merely a glucose transport hormone. It is a growth hormone. When dietary sugar drives chronic hyperinsulinaemia, the body is continuously sending growth signals to every cell with an insulin receptor — including cancer cells, which often overexpress these receptors.

A high-sugar diet does not merely feed cancer. It simultaneously disarms the immune system's primary defence against it.

Heart Disease — Sugar's Most Deadly Partnership

For decades, saturated fat was blamed for heart disease — a hypothesis promoted largely through research funded by the sugar industry, which paid scientists to divert attention from sugar's cardiovascular role. JAMA Internal Medicine (2016)[10] published documents showing the Sugar Research Foundation funded studies in the 1960s specifically designed to exonerate sugar and blame fat. The actual mechanism of heart disease is now well established: it is primarily inflammatory, and sugar — not dietary fat — is its primary dietary driver.

How Sugar Drives Cardiovascular Disease

  • Triglycerides: Excess dietary sugar — particularly fructose — is converted to triglycerides in the liver (de novo lipogenesis). Elevated triglycerides are now recognised as a stronger cardiovascular risk factor than LDL cholesterol in most populations
  • Small dense LDL: High sugar diets specifically increase the proportion of small, dense LDL particles — the subtype most capable of penetrating arterial walls and initiating atherosclerotic plaque formation
  • Arterial inflammation: Sugar-driven AGEs activate RAGE receptors on arterial endothelial cells, triggering an inflammatory response that initiates and accelerates plaque formation
  • Endothelial dysfunction: High glucose directly impairs the production of nitric oxide by endothelial cells — nitric oxide is the primary vasodilating compound that maintains arterial flexibility. Without it, blood pressure rises and arterial stiffness increases
  • Uric acid: Fructose metabolism uniquely generates uric acid — which directly impairs endothelial nitric oxide synthesis, raises blood pressure, and promotes gout and kidney disease simultaneously
📊 Research — JAMA Internal Medicine (2014)

Adults consuming 17–21% of calories from added sugar had a[9] 38% higher risk of dying from cardiovascular disease compared to those consuming 8% or less. The association held across all demographics, independent of weight, physical activity, and overall diet quality. This was a 15-year prospective study — not correlation, but mortality tracked over time.

Seed Oils and Oxidised LDL — The Missing Link

Oxidised LDL — not simply elevated LDL — is the specific form that deposits in arterial walls and initiates plaque. Dietary oxidised fats from heated seed oils directly produce oxidised LDL in circulation. The combination of high-sugar diet (driving small dense LDL) and high seed oil diet (providing oxidised lipids) creates conditions for accelerated atherosclerosis that neither factor produces alone. Processed food delivers both simultaneously in every serving.

The original hygiene hypothesis proposed that reduced childhood infection exposure produced hypersensitive immune systems. If there is a single mechanism that unifies the modern chronic disease epidemic — cancer, heart disease, autoimmunity, Alzheimer's, metabolic disease, mental health conditions, and hormonal disorders — it is leaky gut syndrome (intestinal hyperpermeability). The gut lining is a single-cell-thick barrier separating the body from the trillions of bacteria in the intestinal lumen. When this barrier is breached, bacteria, endotoxins, and undigested food particles enter the bloodstream continuously — triggering the chronic immune activation and inflammatory signalling that underlies every major modern disease.

The LPS Cascade — Bacterial Endotoxin as Disease Driver

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria — the most common bacteria in the gut. When it crosses a leaky gut barrier into the bloodstream, it is recognised by TLR4 receptors on immune cells as a potent danger signal. This activates NF-κB — the master inflammatory transcription factor — in liver cells, fat cells, arterial endothelial cells, brain microglia, and immune cells throughout the body simultaneously. This is metabolic endotoxaemia — the chronic, low-grade LPS exposure from leaky gut that research increasingly identifies as the foundational driver of insulin resistance, Alzheimer's, depression, cardiovascular disease, and cancer progression.

The Chemical Mechanisms — How Processed Meat Causes Cancer

1. N-Nitroso Compounds (NOCs)

Nitrites and nitrates used as preservatives in processed meat react with the haem iron in meat (and with amino acids at high temperatures) to form N-nitroso compounds — a class of potent carcinogens. NOCs directly alkylate (chemically modify) DNA in colon cells, producing mutations that initiate cancer. The colon is the primary site because this is where the fermentation of meat occurs. Research confirms that people eating high processed meat diets have measurably higher NOC excretion in urine and higher DNA adducts in colon cells.

2. Haem Iron — Free Radical Production in the Colon

Haem iron — the form of iron found in red and processed meat — catalyses the production of free radicals in the colon environment. These reactive oxygen species damage colon cell DNA and membranes. Plant-based iron (non-haem iron) does not carry this risk — it is absorbed through different pathways and does not catalyse the same radical chemistry. This is one reason why high iron intake from supplements does not carry the same colorectal cancer risk as high intake from meat.

3. Heterocyclic Amines (HCAs) and Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)

When meat is cooked at high temperatures — grilling, frying, braaiing — amino acids and creatine react to form heterocyclic amines. When fat drips onto coals or a hot surface, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons form in the smoke that coats the meat. Both HCAs and PAHs are potent mutagens that form DNA adducts in multiple tissues. The more well-done, charred, or smoked the meat, the higher the HCA and PAH load.

The European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition — 500,000 participants across 10 European countries followed for 10 years — found that processed meat consumption was[20] positively associated with colorectal cancer, stomach cancer, and bladder cancer. The association for colorectal cancer was dose-dependent, linear, and held after adjustment for all confounding variables including total caloric intake, physical activity, and alcohol consumption.

TMAO — The Hidden Cardiovascular Mechanism

One of the most significant recent discoveries in nutrition science is the trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) pathway. When gut bacteria metabolise L-carnitine (found in red meat) and choline (found in all animal products), they produce trimethylamine — which the liver converts to TMAO. TMAO directly promotes cholesterol deposition in arterial walls, impairs reverse cholesterol transport, and activates the inflammatory signalling in arterial endothelial cells that initiates atherosclerosis. Research published in the New England Journal of Medicine (2013)[21] found that TMAO levels predicted cardiovascular disease risk independently of all traditional risk factors. Plant-based dieters produce virtually no TMAO — because they lack the gut bacteria that perform the conversion, having been outcompeted by non-TMAO-producing species on a plant-rich diet.

The Biological Design Problem

Pigs have a single-stomach digestive system with rapid gastric emptying (4–6 hours). A pig's simple gut lacks this extended detoxification — absorbing material with far less pathogen neutralisation

Pigs have very few functional sweat glands and cannot thermoregulate through perspiration. Cattle have a multi-stage rumen fermentation system that breaks down and neutralises many harmful compounds before they reach the bloodstream. Pigs have a simple monogastric stomach — whatever they eat moves into circulation with minimal filtration

Pigs naturally eat refuse, faeces, carrion, and diseased material. God designed them as environmental cleanup systems — not food. Even when fed controlled diets on modern farms, their biology concentrates pathogens more efficiently than any clean animal
