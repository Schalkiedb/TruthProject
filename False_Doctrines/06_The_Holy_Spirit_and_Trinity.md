# False Doctrine 6: The Trinity Doctrine — What Does Scripture Actually Teach?
## A Study from Scripture

---

## Introduction: The Need for Careful Study

Few doctrines are considered more foundational to mainstream Christianity than the Trinity — the belief that God is one Being existing in three co-equal, co-eternal persons of one substance: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

This study is not written to dismiss the greatness of the Father, the reality of Jesus Christ, or the reality of the Holy Spirit. All three are fully affirmed throughout this study. The question is: **Does the specific formulation of the Trinity doctrine — as defined at Nicaea (325 CE) and Constantinople (381 CE) — actually reflect what Scripture teaches?**

The answer requires a careful, honest engagement with the Bible's actual language.

> "To the law and to the testimony! If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them." (Isaiah 8:20, NKJV)

---

## Part 1: The Historical Development of the Trinity Doctrine

The Trinitarian formula was NOT the universal, uncontested belief of the early church. It developed through decades of controversy and was defined by councils deeply influenced by political pressure.

**Key Timeline**:
- **1st–2nd century CE**: Early Christian writings name the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit together (for example, the Didache 7:1 — see Part 6.2), but without the later language of "one substance" or "three persons in one being"
- **325 CE — Council of Nicaea**: Emperor Constantine convened this council primarily to resolve the Arian controversy. The term *homoousios* ("same substance") was adopted to define the relationship of the Son to the Father — though this specific word does not appear in Scripture. Constantine, not yet baptized, opened the council and took part in its debates
- **381 CE — Council of Constantinople**: Expanded the creed's statement on the Holy Spirit — "the Lord and Giver of life... who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" — completing the formula later summed up as three persons in one substance
- **589 CE — Third Council of Toledo**: Added the *filioque* clause to the Nicene Creed — "the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father AND the Son" — a lasting dispute between East and West, and one of the causes of their final split in 1054, alongside disputes over the authority of the pope

**The word "Trinity" (Latin: *Trinitas*)** does not appear in Scripture. It was coined by Tertullian (~200 CE) and systematized over the following centuries.

## Part 2: Who Is God the Father? — The Biblical Answer

### 2.1 The Shema — Israel's Central Confession

> "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is **one**." (Deuteronomy 6:4, NKJV)

Jesus Himself affirmed this as the first and greatest commandment:

> "Jesus answered him, '**The first of all the commandments is: "Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one."**'" (Mark 12:29, NKJV)

### 2.2 One God = The Father

> "Yet for us there is **one God, the Father**, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist." (1 Corinthians 8:6, ESV)

Paul explicitly identifies the "one God" as **the Father** — a distinct person from "one Lord, Jesus Christ." These are not interchangeable.

> "There is **one God and Father of all**, who is above all, and through all, and in you all." (Ephesians 4:6, NKJV)

> "...the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent." (John 17:3, NKJV — Jesus praying to the Father)

Jesus distinguishes between Himself and "the only true God" who is His Father. He is not calling Himself "the only true God" here — He is addressing His Father by that title.

### 2.3 Yet Scripture Also Calls the Son God

"One God, the Father" names the Father as the source "of whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6, KJV). But Scripture does not stop there. It also calls the Son God:

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and **the Word was God**." (**John 1:1**, KJV)

> "But unto the Son he saith, **Thy throne, O God**, is for ever and ever: a sceptre of righteousness is the sceptre of thy kingdom." (**Hebrews 1:8**, KJV) — here the Father Himself addresses the Son as God.

> "And Thomas answered and said unto him, **My Lord and my God**." (**John 20:28**, KJV) — and Jesus accepted it.

And lying to the Holy Ghost is called lying to God (**Acts 5:3-4**; see Part 4.1).

Both statements are Scripture: there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things; and the Son is God, sharing the Father's divine nature (Colossians 2:9; see Part 6.3). Scripture holds both together without explaining *how* in philosophical terms. This study does the same, and does not supply the explanation that Scripture leaves out.

## Part 3: Who Is Jesus Christ? — The Biblical Answer

Scripture consistently presents Jesus as:
1. Truly divine — sharing the Father's nature
2. Truly distinct — a separate person from the Father
3. Subordinate in role — the Son sent by the Father

### 3.1 Jesus Is Genuinely Divine

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and **the Word was God**. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through Him, and without Him nothing was made that was made." (John 1:1-3, NKJV)

> "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory, the glory as of the **only begotten of the Father**, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14, NKJV)

> "For in Him dwells all the **fullness of the Godhead bodily**." (Colossians 2:9, NKJV)

His life is His own, not received from another:

> "In Christ is life, original, unborrowed, underived." — Ellen G. White, *The Desire of Ages*, p. 530

### 3.2 Jesus Is Distinct from the Father

> "And the Word was **with** God." (John 1:1, NKJV) — "with" (Greek *pros*) implies face-to-face distinction

> "Who, being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal with God, but made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant, and **coming in the likeness of men**." (Philippians 2:6-7, NKJV)

> "I came forth from the **Father** and have come into the world. Again, I leave the world and go to the **Father**." (John 16:28, NKJV)

> "For God so loved the world that He gave **His only begotten Son**..." (John 3:16, NKJV)

A Son must have a Father who is distinct from him.

### 3.3 Jesus Is Subordinate in Role to the Father

These passages are not diminishments of Christ's nature — they reflect the relationship between Father and Son:

> "**The Father is greater than I.**" (John 14:28, NKJV)

> "But about that day or hour no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, **nor the Son, but only the Father.**" (Matthew 24:36, NIV; compare Mark 13:32)

> "I can of **Myself do nothing**... because I do not seek My own will but the will of **the Father who sent Me.**" (John 5:30, NKJV)

> "Now when all things are made subject to Him, then the **Son Himself will also be subject to Him** who put all things under Him, that God may be all in all." (1 Corinthians 15:28, NKJV)

**These verses describe role and order, not nature.** Scripture also says plainly that the Son is equal with God:

> "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be **equal with God**:" (**Philippians 2:6**, KJV)

> "...but said also that God was his Father, **making himself equal with God**." (**John 5:18**, KJV)

So the Son is **equal with the Father in nature** (Philippians 2:6; Colossians 2:9) and **subordinate to the Father in role**: sent by Him, doing His will, and finally "subject unto him" (1 Corinthians 15:28). The difficulty with the creeds is not the word "equal." It is the claim that equality must be defined as "one substance," and that the persons are equal in every sense, which leaves no room for the order of Father and Son that these verses describe.

**An honest note**: Trinitarians usually explain John 14:28 and Matthew 24:36 by Christ's human nature — He "made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7, KJV). That explanation fits some of these texts. It is harder to apply to 1 Corinthians 15:28, which describes the Son's subjection to the Father *after* His work is finished, at the end of the age. Trinitarians usually explain that verse as Christ, in His role as Mediator, handing the kingdom back to the Father.

## Part 4: Who Is the Holy Spirit? — The Biblical Answer

Scripture speaks of the Holy Spirit in two ways, and both must be held together:

1. He is **the Spirit of God and the Spirit of Christ** — the one through whom the Father and the Son dwell in believers.
2. He **acts as a person** — He speaks, calls, forbids, decides, intercedes, can be grieved, and can be lied to.

Rejecting the Nicene formula does not require denying the second point. The question this study raises about the creeds is their "one substance" metaphysics, not whether the Holy Spirit is a person. *(Verses in this part are quoted from the King James Version.)*

### 4.1 The Spirit Acts as a Person

> **Acts 13:2** (KJV) — "As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them."

The Spirit speaks in the first person: "separate *me*," "*I* have called them."

> **Acts 16:6-7** (KJV) — "...and were forbidden of the Holy Ghost to preach the word in Asia, After they were come to Mysia, they assayed to go into Bithynia: but the Spirit suffered them not."

> **Acts 15:28** (KJV) — "For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things;"

The council places the Spirit's decision beside its own: "to the Holy Ghost, and to us."

> **1 Corinthians 12:11** (KJV) — "But all these worketh that one and the selfsame Spirit, dividing to every man severally as he will."

> **Romans 8:27** (KJV) — "And he that searcheth the hearts knoweth what is the mind of the Spirit, because he maketh intercession for the saints according to the will of God."

> **Ephesians 4:30** (KJV) — "And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption."

A will, a mind, intercession, and grief are personal. A power or an influence cannot be grieved.

> **Acts 5:3-4** (KJV) — "But Peter said, Ananias, why hath Satan filled thine heart to lie to the Holy Ghost... thou hast not lied unto men, but unto God."

Lying to the Holy Ghost is lying to God.

**An honest note**: the KJV sometimes says "the Spirit itself" (Romans 8:16, 26) because the Greek word *pneuma* is grammatically neuter. Grammatical gender does not decide the question either way. The case for the Spirit's personhood rests on what He does — speaking, deciding, willing, interceding, being grieved — not on pronouns.

### 4.2 He Is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son

> **Romans 8:9** (KJV) — "But ye are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit, if so be that the Spirit of God dwell in you. Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his."

> **Matthew 10:20** (KJV) — "For it is not ye that speak, but the Spirit of your Father which speaketh in you."

**The Spirit of God** and **the Spirit of Christ** are used of the same Spirit in a single sentence. He belongs to both the Father and the Son, and through Him both dwell in believers:

> **Ephesians 3:17** (KJV) — "That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith;"

> **Colossians 1:27** (KJV) — "...Christ in you, the hope of glory:"

> **Galatians 2:20** (KJV) — "...nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me:"

### 4.3 How Both Fit Together: Christ's Representative

Jesus promised the Spirit in words that hold both points at once:

> **John 14:16-18** (KJV) — "And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever; Even the Spirit of truth... I will not leave you comfortless: I will come to you."

The Spirit is "another Comforter" — distinct from Jesus, a second helper of the same kind. And yet through Him, Jesus Himself comes: "I will come to you."

Ellen White explains the relationship this way:

> "The Holy Spirit is Christ's representative, but divested of the personality of humanity, and independent thereof. Cumbered with humanity, Christ could not be in every place personally. Therefore it was for their interest that He should go to the Father, and send the Spirit to be His successor on earth." — *The Desire of Ages*, p. 669

Christ, having taken human nature, is in one place. The Spirit, not limited by a human body, is everywhere, and through Him Christ is present with every believer.

### 4.4 The Spirit Speaks What He Hears

> **John 16:13-14** (KJV) — "Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth: for he shall not speak of himself; but whatsoever he shall hear, that shall he speak: and he will shew you things to come. He shall glorify me: for he shall receive of mine, and shall shew it unto you."

This passage shows order and harmony within the Godhead, not the absence of personhood. Jesus says the same of Himself: "I can of mine own self do nothing... because I seek not mine own will, but the will of the Father which hath sent me" (**John 5:30**, KJV). The Son does not speak on His own initiative, and He is a person. The same is true of the Spirit. Notice too that John uses "he" for the Spirit throughout (John 16:13-14), though *pneuma* is neuter — the writer chose the personal pronoun.

### 4.5 Ellen White on the Holy Spirit

Ellen White's writings say plainly that the Spirit is a divine person, and they also refuse to speculate about His nature.

**The Third Person of the Godhead:**

> "Sin could be resisted and overcome only through the mighty agency of the Third Person of the Godhead, who would come with no modified energy, but in the fullness of divine power." — *The Desire of Ages*, p. 671

**Three powers working together:**

> "In the great closing work we shall meet with perplexities that we know not how to deal with; but let us not forget that the three great powers of heaven are working, that a divine hand is on the wheel, and that God will bring His promises to pass." — *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 8, p. 254

**A warning against speculation:**

> "The nature of the Holy Spirit is a mystery. Men cannot explain it, because the Lord has not revealed it to them... Regarding such mysteries, which are too deep for human understanding, silence is golden." — *The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 52

She affirms that the Spirit is the Third Person of the Godhead, working in the fullness of divine power, while refusing to define *how* the three are related. That is the opposite of the creeds, which define the "how" in philosophical terms. The word "Trinity" and the "one substance" formula do not appear in any of the 22 Ellen White books held on this site.

**Her explicit statements on the Spirit's personhood** are gathered in *Evangelism* (pp. 615-617, compiled 1946). Each sentence below has been checked against the EGW Writings database, with the original source she wrote it in:

> "We need to realize that the Holy Spirit, who is as much a person as God is a person, is walking through these grounds." — Manuscript 66, 1899, a talk to the students at Avondale (*Evangelism*, p. 616)

> "The Holy Spirit is a person, for He beareth witness with our spirits that we are the children of God." — *Evangelism*, p. 616 (also *20 Manuscript Releases*, p. 68)

> "The Holy Spirit has a personality, else He could not bear witness to our spirits and with our spirits that we are the children of God." — *Evangelism*, p. 617 (also *20 Manuscript Releases*, p. 69)

> "The prince of the power of evil can only be held in check by the power of God in the third person of the Godhead, the Holy Spirit." — *Special Testimonies*, Series A, No. 10, p. 37 (1897) (*Evangelism*, p. 617)

> "There are three living persons of the heavenly trio." — *Special Testimonies*, Series B, No. 7, p. 63 (*Evangelism*, p. 615)

> "We are to co-operate with the three highest powers in heaven,—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost..." — *Special Testimonies*, Series B, No. 7, p. 51 (*Evangelism*, p. 617)

> "...the eternal heavenly dignitaries—God, and Christ, and the Holy Spirit..." — *16 Manuscript Releases*, p. 204 (*Evangelism*, p. 616)

**A note on the word "Trinity" in *Evangelism***: the bold headings in *Evangelism* were written by the 1946 compilers, not by Ellen White. On page 616 the compilers' heading reads "The Eternal Dignitaries of the Trinity," but her own sentence beneath it says "God, and Christ, and the Holy Spirit." When reading compilations, quote her words, not the headings.

### 4.6 The Pioneers

The early Adventist pioneers rejected the Trinity formula of the creeds. Ellen White's writings, including *The Desire of Ages* (1898), call the Holy Spirit "the Third Person of the Godhead" while never adopting the creeds' formula. This study holds the same two positions: it rejects the "one substance" metaphysics of Nicaea and Constantinople, and it affirms the personhood of the Holy Spirit that Scripture describes (4.1).

## Part 5: The Apostolic Greetings — What They Show

Paul opens every one of his letters with grace and peace from the Father and the Son:

> "Grace to you and peace from **God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ**." (Romans 1:7, KJV; the same greeting in 1 Corinthians 1:3; 2 Corinthians 1:2; Galatians 1:3; Ephesians 1:2; Philippians 1:2; Colossians 1:2; 1 Thessalonians 1:1; 2 Thessalonians 1:2; 1 Timothy 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:2; Titus 1:4; Philemon 1:3)

> "Grace and peace from **God the Father and from our Lord Jesus Christ**, the Son of the Father..." (2 John 1:3, NKJV)

These greetings name the Father and the Son as the **source** of grace. They fit the way Scripture describes the Spirit as the one **through whom** that grace reaches the believer: "the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us" (**Romans 5:5**, KJV).

But the greetings cannot be used to argue that the Spirit is not a person, because the Spirit is named alongside the Father and the Son elsewhere in the same kind of blessing:

> "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the **communion of the Holy Ghost**, be with you all. Amen." (**2 Corinthians 13:14**, KJV)

> "Grace be unto you, and peace, from him which is, and which was, and which is to come; and **from the seven Spirits which are before his throne**; And from Jesus Christ, who is the faithful witness..." (**Revelation 1:4-5**, KJV)

In Revelation 1:4-5, grace and peace come from three: the Father, "the seven Spirits," and Jesus Christ. "Seven" in Revelation is the number of fullness, and the "seven Spirits of God" are "sent forth into all the earth" (**Revelation 5:6**).

**An honest note**: most readers take "the seven Spirits" as the Holy Spirit in His fullness; a minority read them as seven angels. On either reading, Revelation 1:4-5 is not a greeting from the Father and the Son alone.

## Part 6: The Textual Problems — Verses Used to Prove The Trinity

### 6.1 The Johannine Comma — 1 John 5:7 (KJV)

> "For there are **three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one**." (1 John 5:7, KJV)

This is the most explicit Trinitarian statement in the New Testament — and it is **a later addition, not part of what John wrote**. The words in bold, known as the "Johannine Comma," are not found in the Greek text of the early church.

**The facts**:
- **Greek manuscripts**: the words are found in fewer than ten Greek manuscripts, all of them late. The earliest that has them in the text itself is from the 14th century (minuscule 629). In several others they appear only as a note in the margin. None of the hundreds of other Greek manuscripts of 1 John, including all the early ones, contains them.
- **Greek church fathers**: none quotes the passage in the Trinitarian debates of the 4th and 5th centuries, when it would have been the strongest proof text available.
- **First appearance**: it first appears in Latin, in a late 4th-century treatise, the *Liber Apologeticus* (c. 380), attributed to Priscillian or his follower Instantius. It entered Latin Bible manuscripts between about the 5th and 7th centuries; some major early Latin Bibles, such as Codex Fuldensis (546), still lack it in the text.
- **Erasmus**: he left it out of the first two editions of his Greek New Testament (1516, 1519) because his Greek manuscripts did not have it. He added it in the third edition (1522), after critics accused him of favouring Arianism and he was shown a Greek manuscript containing it (minuscule 61, copied around 1520). He recorded his doubts about it in his *Annotations*. The popular story that he *promised* to include it if one Greek manuscript were found has no documentary evidence, and Bruce Metzger removed it from later editions of his textbook.
- **Modern translations**: all major ones (NIV, ESV, NASB, RSV, NRSV, NLT, CSB) omit it or footnote it as a later addition.

**How it most likely arose**: as an interpretive note on verse 8 — reading "the Spirit, and the water, and the blood" as a figure of the Father, the Word, and the Spirit — which a copyist later took into the Latin text. Whether that first happened by mistake or on purpose cannot be known. What can be known is that it is not original, and it should not be used as a proof text for any position.

*Sources*: Bruce M. Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, on 1 John 5:7-8; Bruce M. Metzger and Bart D. Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament* (4th ed., Oxford University Press, 2005), p. 146 (Erasmus).

### 6.2 Matthew 28:19 — "In the Name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit"

> "Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them **in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit**." (Matthew 28:19, NKJV)

This verse is genuine. Observe several points:

**1. The word is "name," singular, not "names."** Three are named, and they share one name. This shows the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit in the work of salvation. It does not require the creeds' "one substance" definition, and it places the Spirit alongside the Father and the Son, not beneath them as a mere force.

**2. In Acts, baptism is described as "in the name of Jesus Christ"** (NKJV):
- Acts 2:38 — "be baptized... **in the name of Jesus Christ**"
- Acts 8:16 — "baptized **in the name of the Lord Jesus**"
- Acts 10:48 — "baptized **in the name of the Lord**"
- Acts 19:5 — "baptized **in the name of the Lord Jesus**"
- Acts 22:16 — "be baptized, and wash away your sins, **calling on the name of the Lord**"

Acts never records the words spoken at a baptism, so it cannot be said that the apostles never used the threefold formula. What Acts shows is that baptism was into the authority of Jesus Christ, which Matthew 28:18-19 itself grounds in His words: "All power is given unto me... Go ye therefore..." (KJV)

**3. The textual question.** Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260–340 CE), who attended the Council of Nicaea, often quoted this verse in a shorter form, "make disciples of all the nations **in My name**." Some have argued from this that the threefold phrase was added later. Two facts weigh heavily against that:

- **The Didache**, a church manual usually dated to the late first or early second century — about two hundred years before Nicaea — already gives the threefold formula: "baptize into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Didache 7:1).
- **Every surviving manuscript of Matthew** that contains this passage includes the threefold phrase.

Eusebius' shorter form is best explained as a paraphrase or abbreviation, which ancient writers often used when quoting from memory. The verse should be treated as original.

### 6.3 "Godhead" in the KJV — Divine Nature, Not a Formula

The KJV uses the word "Godhead" three times, translating three related Greek words:

> "Forasmuch then as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the **Godhead** (*to theion*, "the divine") is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man's device." (Acts 17:29, KJV)

> "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and **Godhead** (*theiotēs*, "divinity")." (Romans 1:20, KJV)

> "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the **Godhead** (*theotēs*) bodily." (Colossians 2:9, KJV)

In each case "Godhead" names **what God is** — His divine nature — not a number of persons or a formula about them.

**Colossians 2:9 is the strongest of the three.** The phrase is *tēs theotētos* (τῆς θεότητος), "of the deity": the state of being God, the divine nature itself (Strong's G2320). It is a stronger word than *theiotēs* in Romans 1:20 (Strong's G2305), which describes divine qualities that can be seen in creation. Paul does not say that something godlike dwells in Christ. He says that **all the fulness of the deity** dwells in Him, in bodily form. (The difference between the two words is set out in R. C. Trench, *Synonyms of the New Testament*, §2.)

This means the Son has **the same divine nature as the Father** — not a portion of it, and not a lesser or borrowed divinity. Scripture says the same in other words:

- "the Word was God" (**John 1:1**, KJV)
- "being in the form of God" (**Philippians 2:6**, KJV)
- "the express image of his person" (**Hebrews 1:3**, KJV)

**How this fits the rest of this study**: Christ shares the Father's divine nature fully (Part 3.1), and He is a distinct person from the Father (Part 3.2). Colossians 2:9 states the first truth in Scripture's own words. What Scripture does not do is go on to define the relationship in the creeds' philosophical terms — "one substance," "consubstantial" — which Rome itself says were developed from philosophy (Part 8.1).

**An honest note**: Trinitarians also cite Colossians 2:9 for the full deity of Christ, and on that point this study agrees with them. The disagreement is not about whether Jesus is fully divine. It is about whether the councils' definition of the Godhead may be added to what Scripture says.

## Part 7: Jesus as Michael the Archangel — A Biblical Case

No verse says in so many words "Jesus is Michael." The identification is drawn from the roles Scripture gives to Michael, which match the roles it gives to Christ. *(Verses in this part are quoted from the King James Version.)*

**"Michael" means "Who is like God?"** — a fitting name for the Son who bears God's image and nature.

**"Archangel"** (*archangelos*) means **chief of the angels** — the one who rules over them — not one angel among many. The name describes His office as Commander of heaven's armies, not His nature.

### Biblical Evidence

**1. Michael, the Prince who stands for God's people**:

> "...and there is none that holdeth with me in these things, but **Michael your prince**." (**Daniel 10:21**, KJV)

> "And at that time shall **Michael stand up, the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people**: and there shall be a time of trouble, such as never was since there was a nation even to that same time: and at that time thy people shall be delivered..." (**Daniel 12:1**, KJV)

Michael "stands up" at the close of history, and God's people are delivered. Daniel uses the same title, "Prince," for the Messiah: "Messiah the Prince" (**Daniel 9:25**, KJV) and "the Prince of princes" (**Daniel 8:25**).

**2. The Captain of the LORD's host, who accepts worship**:

> "And he said, Nay; but as **captain of the host of the LORD** am I now come. And Joshua **fell on his face to the earth, and did worship**, and said unto him, What saith my lord unto his servant? And the captain of the LORD's host said unto Joshua, **Loose thy shoe from off thy foot; for the place whereon thou standest is holy**." (**Joshua 5:14-15**, KJV)

The Commander of the LORD's armies accepts Joshua's worship and declares the ground holy, as God did to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:5). Created angels refuse worship (**Revelation 19:10; 22:8-9**). This Commander is divine. In Revelation 12:7, it is Michael who leads the armies of heaven: "Michael and his angels fought against the dragon."

**3. The voice that raises the dead**:

> "For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, **with the voice of the archangel**, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first:" (**1 Thessalonians 4:16**, KJV)

> "...the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves **shall hear his voice**, and shall come forth;" (**John 5:28-29**, KJV)

In John 5 the voice that raises the dead is Christ's. In 1 Thessalonians 4 the Lord descends "with the voice of the archangel."

**4. Michael contends with Satan**:

> "Yet **Michael the archangel**, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses..." (**Jude 9**, KJV)

Moses was later seen alive with Christ on the mount of transfiguration (**Matthew 17:3**). The one who contended for Moses' body is the one who raises the dead.

### The Objection from Hebrews 1

> "For unto which of the angels said he at any time, **Thou art my Son**, this day have I begotten thee?... And again, when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world, he saith, **And let all the angels of God worship him**." (**Hebrews 1:5-6**, KJV)

Hebrews 1 shows that the Son is not an angel by nature: angels worship Him. This study agrees. Calling Christ "Michael the archangel" does not make Him a created angel. "Archangel" names His office as chief over the angels, just as "Captain of the host" does in Joshua 5. The one the angels worship is the one who leads them.

### Not a New Idea

Identifying Michael with Christ did not begin with Seventh-day Adventists. Protestant interpreters held or allowed it long before; Adventists recovered and reaffirmed it.

- **John Calvin**, the Reformer, on Daniel 12:1: "By Michael many agree in understanding Christ as the head of the Church." Calvin himself allowed that the archangel reading would also fit, and concluded that either way "God was the preserver of his Church by the hand of his only-begotten Son." — *Commentary on Daniel* (1561; English translation by Thomas Myers, 1853), on Daniel 12:1
- **John Gill**, the 18th-century Baptist commentator, on Revelation 12:7, after rejecting the view that Michael is a created angel: "it seems best to interpret it of Jesus Christ, who is equal with God, is his fellow, is one with the Father." — *Exposition of the New Testament*, on Revelation 12:7

Calvin reports the view as widely held in his day without settling on it; Gill states it as his own conclusion.

### Ellen White

Ellen White makes the identification directly when she quotes Daniel 10:21: "there is none that holdeth with me in these things, but Michael [Christ] your Prince" (*The Desire of Ages*, p. 99).

**An honest note**: this is an identification drawn from matching roles — Prince of God's people, Commander of heaven's armies, the voice that raises the dead — not from a verse that states it. Christians who reject it usually read Michael as the highest created angel, pointing to Jude 9, where Michael says "The Lord rebuke thee" rather than rebuking Satan himself. Readers should weigh the texts above for themselves.

## Part 8: What Rome Teaches, and What Protestants Kept

The earlier parts of this study argue from Scripture. This part sets beside them what the churches themselves say, in their own official words.

### 8.1 Rome's Own Definition

The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* places the doctrine at the center of everything:

> "The mystery of the Most Holy Trinity is the central mystery of Christian faith and life." — *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, §234

It defines the doctrine in the language of the councils:

> "We do not confess three Gods, but one God in three persons, the 'consubstantial Trinity'." — *Catechism*, §253

And it states plainly where that language came from:

> "In order to articulate the dogma of the Trinity, the Church had to develop her own terminology with the help of certain notions of philosophical origin: 'substance,' 'person' or 'hypostasis,' 'relation'..." — *Catechism*, §251

The Catechism also calls the Trinity "a mystery of faith in the strict sense" (§237): one that, by its own account, could not be known by reason alone.

**What this shows**: Rome does not claim that the defining terms of the doctrine came from Scripture. By its own statement, the Church developed them, using ideas from philosophy. This is the same point made in Part 6.3: the Bible speaks of the "Godhead" as divine nature, but it never uses "substance" or "consubstantial."

### 8.2 The Athanasian Creed: The Formula Made a Condition of Salvation

The Athanasian Creed, used in both Catholic and Protestant churches, goes further. It makes believing the formula necessary for salvation:

> "Whosoever will be saved: before all things it is necessary that he hold the Catholic Faith:" (v. 1)

> "And the Catholic Faith is this: That we worship one God in Trinity, and Trinity in Unity;" (v. 3)

> "He therefore that will be saved, must thus think of the Trinity." (v. 28)

> "This is the Catholic Faith: which except a man believe faithfully, he can not be saved." (v. 44)

*(English text of 1548, as given in Philip Schaff, *The Creeds of Christendom*, vol. 2.)*

Jesus defined eternal life differently:

> **John 17:3** (KJV) — "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent."

When the jailer asked what he must do to be saved, Paul answered, "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved" (**Acts 16:31**, KJV). Neither Jesus nor Paul made salvation depend on a definition of "substance" and "persons."

**An honest note**: despite its name, the creed was not written by Athanasius. It is a Latin text of the 5th or 6th century.

### 8.3 The Protestant Confessions Kept the Same Formula

The Reformers rejected much of Rome's tradition, but on this doctrine they kept the councils' definition, often word for word.

**Lutheran — the Augsburg Confession (1530), Article I:**

> "The churches, with common consent among us, do teach that the decree of the Nicene Synod concerning the unity of the divine essence and of the three persons is true, and without doubt to be believed..."

The Lutheran confession does not ground the doctrine in a text of Scripture. It grounds it in "the decree of the Nicene Synod" — the Council of Nicaea.

**Anglican — the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571), Article I:**

> "And in unity of this Godhead there be three Persons, of one substance, power, and eternity; the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost."

**Presbyterian — the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), Chapter II, section 3:**

> "In the unity of the Godhead there be three persons, of one substance, power, and eternity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost..."

The same section goes on to say that the Son is "eternally begotten of the Father" and the Holy Ghost is "eternally proceeding from the Father and the Son." That last phrase is the *filioque* — the words "and the Son" added to the creed at the Third Council of Toledo in 589 (see Part 1). The Westminster Confession, written to state Protestant faith from Scripture alone, keeps an addition made by a Catholic council in the 6th century.

### 8.4 The Same Question as the Sabbath

Protestants hold that Scripture alone is the rule of faith. Yet the defining words of their confessions on the Godhead — "one substance," "eternally begotten," "proceeding from the Father and the Son" — come from councils, not from the Bible, and Rome itself says those terms are philosophical in origin (§251). This is the same question this library raises about Sunday: a practice or doctrine received from the councils and kept by Protestants, without a text of Scripture that states it.

> **Mark 7:7** (KJV) — "Howbeit in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men."

> "To the law and to the testimony! If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them." (**Isaiah 8:20**, NKJV)

**What this study does *not* reject**: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; the full divinity of Christ; or the personhood of the Holy Spirit (Part 4). What it rejects is a definition of their relationship written in philosophical terms by councils, and the claim that believing that definition is necessary for salvation.

## Conclusion: What Scripture Actually Teaches

Scripture teaches:
1. **One God, the Father, "of whom are all things"** — the source and final authority (1 Corinthians 8:6, KJV; Deuteronomy 6:4; John 17:3)
2. **Jesus Christ = the only begotten Son** — God by nature, equal with the Father in divinity, distinct in person, subordinate in role (John 1:1; Hebrews 1:8; Colossians 2:9; Philippians 2:6; John 14:28)
3. **The Holy Spirit = the Third Person of the Godhead** — a divine person who speaks, calls, decides and can be grieved (Acts 13:2; 15:28; Ephesians 4:30), the Spirit of the Father and of Christ, through whom the Father and Son dwell in believers (Romans 8:9; John 14:16-18; Colossians 1:27)

This does NOT reduce Christ or dismiss the Holy Spirit. It accurately reflects the language Scripture actually uses, rather than the metaphysical formulas developed by post-biblical councils under political pressure.

The unity of God is not the unity of "three persons in one substance" (a concept Paul and Jesus never articulated). It is the unity of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, one in purpose and in the work of salvation — like the unity of marriage (Genesis 2:24 "they shall become **one flesh**" — distinct persons, truly one). Ellen White describes it the same way:

> "The unity that exists between Christ and His disciples does not destroy the personality of either. They are one in purpose, in mind, in character, but not in person. It is thus that God and Christ are one." — *The Ministry of Healing*, p. 422 (also *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 8, p. 269)

How the three are related beyond what Scripture says is not revealed, and "regarding such mysteries... silence is golden" (AA 52).

> **"Now this is eternal life: that they know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom You have sent."** (John 17:3, NIV)

The path to eternal life runs through knowing the Father as the "only true God" and Jesus as His sent Son — exactly as Jesus Himself defined it.
